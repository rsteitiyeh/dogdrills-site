A puppy crate training schedule starts with 10-minute open-door sessions at 8 weeks, builds to 30-minute lock-ins by week two, and reaches reliable 3-4 hour holds by month three. The ceiling: one hour per month of age, plus one. Stay inside that limit and most puppies accept the crate as a rest spot within three weeks. Print or save this page if you want a puppy crate training schedule PDF reference.

That ceiling is not the target. A 9-week-old who just ate and drank might need out in 45 minutes. A 10-week-old crashed after a hard play session may go 90 minutes without complaint. The schedule moves by reading the dog, not by following an arbitrary timer. When the current session length is consistently calm, extend it. When there are stress signals or accidents, pull back one step and hold there for three days.

## How long can a puppy stay in the crate by age?

Less than most owners expect. The American Kennel Club's training guidance places the practical maximum at one hour per month of age for puppies under 6 months. Overnight holds run longer because puppies slow their metabolism during sleep and naturally suppress the urge to toilet ([AKC crate training guide](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/training/crate-training-puppy-schedule/)).

| Age | Max Daytime Hold | Max Overnight |
|---|---|---|
| 8-10 weeks | 1-2 hours | 3-4 hours |
| 10-12 weeks | 2-3 hours | 4-5 hours |
| 3 months | 3 hours | 5-6 hours |
| 4 months | 4 hours | 6-7 hours |
| 5 months | 4-5 hours | 7 hours |
| 6 months+ | up to 6 hours | through the night |

Overnight tolerance develops faster than daytime tolerance, and most guides never explain why. Slow-wave sleep suppresses the urge to toilet. A 10-week puppy who struggles past 90 minutes during the day will often sleep 4 hours overnight without trouble. Count daytime and overnight limits separately or you will misread your puppy's progress.

## What does a week-by-week crate training schedule look like?

The table below is the complete progression from arrival day through month four. Use it as your printable reference.

| Week | Age | Session Length | Overnight | Milestone to Hit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 8-9 wks | Open door only | Crate beside bed | Enters voluntarily |
| 2 | 9-10 wks | 10-15 min | 3-4 hrs (1-2 wake-ups) | Settles within 2 min |
| 3 | 10-11 wks | 20-30 min | 4 hrs (1 wake-up) | Calm when you leave the room |
| 4 | 11-12 wks | 45 min | 4-5 hrs | First out-of-house sessions |
| 5-8 | 3 months | 2-3 hrs | 5-6 hrs | Half-workday stretches |
| 9-12 | 4 months | 3-4 hrs | 6-7 hrs | Crate relocatable |
| 16+ | 5-6 months | 4-5 hrs | Through the night | Full overnight |

**Week 1 (8-9 weeks):** The door stays open all day. Scatter kibble and treats inside. Feed every meal in the crate with the door unlatched. The goal for the entire week: she walks in voluntarily without coaxing.

**Week 2 (9-10 weeks):** Close the door while she finishes a meal, then open it immediately. After two successful meals, try 10-minute lock-ins with you sitting in the room. End the week at 15 minutes. Night sessions go beside your bed so you hear the first whimper and get her out before she panics. Expect one or two toilet runs before 6 am. Treat them as toilet runs, nothing else.

**Week 3 (10-11 weeks):** Extend to 20-30 minutes. She should settle within 60 seconds of the door closing. If she still cries consistently at the door, you moved too fast. Drop back one step. Start leaving the room during sessions. If she stays quiet, extend your time out of sight by 5 minutes each session.

**Week 4 (11-12 weeks):** First sessions where you actually leave the house. Keep them under 45 minutes. If she naps through it without complaint, add 15 minutes every two days. Overnight holds reach 4-5 hours for most puppies at this stage.

**Weeks 5-8 (3 months):** The puppy can manage a half-workday stretch, but she needs a 20-30 minute exercise session and a toilet break before any crate session over 2 hours. A physically tired puppy settles in 60 seconds. An understimulated one fights for 10 minutes.

**Month 4 onward:** Around 16-20 weeks, brief unsupervised time in a puppy-proofed room becomes realistic. Keep the crate available and unlocked as a rest spot through at least 12-18 months. Dogs who lose crate access entirely before their second year are more likely to develop destructive habits when left alone.

## What should a daily crate schedule look like for a young puppy?

For an 8-10 week puppy, here is a full day laid out hour by hour. Young puppies need 16-18 hours of sleep per day. The schedule uses that sleep budget as free training time.

7:00 am: Out of the crate, straight outside to toilet, then breakfast.
8:00 am: Active play and exploration indoors.
9:00 am: Crate for morning nap, 30-45 minutes.
9:45 am: Outside for a toilet break.
10:00 am: Play or calm interaction.
11:00 am: Crate nap, 30-45 minutes.
11:45 am: Outside.
12:00 pm: Lunch in the crate, door open.
12:30 pm: Crate for midday nap, up to 60 minutes.
1:30 pm: Outside, then play or training.
3:00 pm: Crate nap, 45-60 minutes.
3:45 pm: Outside.
4:00 pm: Play or short training session.
5:00 pm: Dinner in the crate, door open.
5:30 pm: Active play, calm down period.
7:00 pm: Crate session, 30-45 minutes.
8:30 pm: Calm family time.
10:00 pm: Last outside trip.
10:30 pm: Crate for the night.

By month three, midday naps consolidate and session lengths stretch. Most owners run three or four crate periods per day at that point rather than seven. Adjust by watching when the puppy gets drowsy and routing that drowsiness into the crate rather than letting her crash on the couch.

## What are the most common crate training mistakes?

**Closing the door before she volunteers to enter.** If you are coaxing the puppy in every session, the association is already negative. Wait for three consecutive voluntary entries before you close the door for the first time. This is not optional if you want a dog who walks into the crate calmly for the next 10 years.

**Crating immediately after heavy exercise.** A puppy with an elevated heart rate from running will fight crating for 15-20 minutes. Wait 10-15 minutes after play for her to come down, then crate. The session goes from a battle to a 60-second settle.

**Using the crate as punishment.** One march to the crate after a scolding puts weeks of positive association at risk. The crate is for sleep and rest. Keep it completely separate from anything negative.

**Overnight crate in a separate room too early.** An 8-week puppy just left her littermates. She has never slept alone. Placing the crate in the kitchen isolates her at the hardest possible moment. Two weeks beside your bed costs almost nothing and eliminates most nighttime crying before it becomes a habit.

**Opening the door during crying.** Wait for 2-3 seconds of quiet before releasing. Opening during active protest teaches her that crying is the door mechanism. The exception: a puppy who was quiet, then starts crying with clear urgency after 60-90 minutes likely needs to toilet. Take her out in complete silence, let her go, and return her with zero fanfare. That pattern is biological necessity, not manipulation.

The ASPCA notes that premature removal of crate access is one of the leading causes of housetraining regression in dogs between 6 and 18 months ([ASPCA crate training guide](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/dog-care/crate-training-your-dog)). The crate does not stop being useful once the puppy is reliable; it stops being necessary sometime between 12 and 24 months depending on the individual dog.

## How long does crate training take?

Most puppies reach reliable crate acceptance in 3-6 weeks with a gradual introduction. Reliable means: enters without protest, settles within 2 minutes, and stays quiet for age-appropriate holds. Full overnight reliability typically lands around weeks 8-10.

Breed and individual variation matters here:

- Toy breeds have smaller bladders and shorter overnight capacity well into month four. Adjust your overnight expectations down.
- Herding and working breeds take to the crate fast structurally, but need real physical and mental output before they will settle in it.
- Brachycephalic breeds need good airflow in the crate at all times. Do not put a heavy blanket over the front panel.

Veterinary behaviorists consistently list early crate training as one of the primary preventative tools for separation anxiety. A dog who learned before 16 weeks that confinement is temporary and predictable handles being alone at 2 years far better than one who never had that foundation. The schedule is worth running even if your lifestyle rarely requires a crated dog.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Should I put water in the crate overnight?**

Pull water 2 hours before bedtime for puppies under 3 months. This cuts overnight toilet trips significantly without dehydrating the puppy. At 3 months and older, a small spill-proof bowl or a lick bottle attached to the crate door is fine overnight. During the day, always offer water outside the crate every 1-2 hours.

**What size crate does a puppy need?**

Big enough to stand, turn around, and lie down fully stretched. Nothing larger. Extra space lets the puppy use one corner as a toilet and sleep away from the mess, which breaks the biological instinct that makes crate training work. Buy a crate with a divider panel if you want one crate for the dog's full life; shrink it now and expand as the puppy grows.

**Is it OK to cover the crate with a blanket?**

Yes, and most puppies settle faster in a covered crate because it reduces stimulation and mimics a den. Leave the front panel open for airflow and visibility. A cover also blocks visual triggers like people walking past or outdoor activity that would otherwise set off barking during rest sessions.

**Can you crate train a puppy while working full time?**

You can, but a standard 8-hour work day is far beyond what any puppy under 4 months can handle physically. A 3-month-old's maximum is around 3 hours. Build in a midday break from a sitter or dog walker for the first 3-4 months. Puppies held in a crate for full work days develop anxiety and fall behind on housetraining by weeks. This is not a schedule problem; it is a biology problem.
