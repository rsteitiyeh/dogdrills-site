The direct answer: for an adult dog peeing indoors, rule out a medical cause first, then reset the training with the same core method used for puppies, a strict outdoor schedule, immediate reward for the right spot, and management that prevents unsupervised indoor access, since an adult dog with an established indoor habit needs the same consistency a puppy does, just applied to an existing pattern instead of a blank slate. Most adult dogs show clear improvement within 2 to 3 weeks once a medical cause is ruled out and the schedule is followed strictly.

## Rule out medical causes before assuming it's behavioral

A previously house-trained adult dog that suddenly starts having accidents is different from a dog that was never fully trained. Urinary tract infections, bladder stones, kidney disease, diabetes, and age-related incontinence can all cause sudden indoor accidents in a dog with no prior history of the problem, and no amount of retraining fixes a medical issue. Any sudden change in an adult dog's house-training reliability warrants a veterinary visit before starting a behavioral reset.

## Why some adult dogs were never fully trained

Dogs adopted from shelters, rescues, or situations with inconsistent early handling sometimes reach adulthood without ever having a truly consistent house-training schedule. These dogs aren't "stubborn" or "slow learners," they simply never had the consistent schedule-and-reward pattern that builds the habit, and they respond to the same method that works for puppies, just often faster since their bladders are fully developed.

## The reset method for adults

Go back to a tighter schedule than you'd think an adult dog needs: outside first thing in the morning, after meals, after naps, after play, and every 3-4 hours during the day for at least the first two weeks. Reward immediately and enthusiastically for eliminating in the correct outdoor spot. Manage every unsupervised moment with a crate, a gate, or a leash tether, since giving an adult dog with a habit free run of the house during the reset undoes the whole plan the first time an accident happens unnoticed.

## Cleaning matters more with adult dogs

Adult dogs that have soiled the same indoor spots repeatedly have a much stronger scent association drawing them back to those locations than a puppy would. Use an enzymatic cleaner specifically designed to break down urine proteins, not a standard household cleaner or one that contains ammonia, which can actually smell similar to urine to a dog's nose and reinforce the spot rather than eliminating the draw.

## Behavioral versus marking

Distinguish between a dog that's fully emptying its bladder indoors (a house-training gap) and a dog leaving small amounts on vertical surfaces or specific objects (marking behavior, more common in intact males but seen in females and neutered dogs too). Marking often has a different trigger, new items in the home, other animals' scent, stress, and may need a combination of the same schedule-based retraining plus addressing the specific trigger, sometimes alongside neutering if the dog is intact.

## Quick differential table

| Sign | Likely cause | Next step |
|---|---|---|
| Sudden accidents, previously reliable | Possible medical issue | Vet visit before behavioral retraining |
| Never fully reliable since adoption | Incomplete original training | Full schedule-based reset |
| Small amounts on vertical surfaces | Marking behavior | Address trigger, consider neutering, same schedule reset |
| Accidents only when left alone | Possible anxiety-related | Assess for separation anxiety alongside house-training |
| Accidents during excitement or greeting | Excitement urination | Manage greeting intensity, avoid punishment |

## Excitement urination: a different problem entirely

Some adult dogs, often smaller breeds, urinate involuntarily during excited greetings or when anxious, which is a physiological response, not a house-training failure. Punishing this makes it worse by adding anxiety to an already involuntary response. Keep greetings low-key, avoid direct eye contact and reaching over the dog's head during initial greeting, and let the dog approach at its own pace, which usually reduces the behavior over time.

## Setting realistic expectations for senior dogs

Senior dogs sometimes develop age-related incontinence or cognitive changes that reduce their ability to signal or hold urine reliably, even after a lifetime of solid house-training. A behavioral reset alone won't resolve a physical or cognitive cause, so a veterinary workup, including bloodwork and sometimes imaging, is the right first step for any senior dog with new-onset accidents before assuming the dog simply needs more training.

## A note on patience during the reset

The hardest week of any adult retraining reset is usually the first one, when old habits still feel automatic to the dog and the new schedule hasn't yet built enough repetitions to override it. Owners who stick with the strict schedule through that rocky first week, even when an accident still slips through, see the pattern shift noticeably by week two. Giving up on the schedule after a single setback is the most common reason a reset takes longer than it needs to.

## FAQ

**Why has my previously house-trained adult dog started having accidents?**
Sudden regression in a previously reliable adult dog is most often medical: urinary tract infections, bladder stones, or age-related incontinence are common causes. Rule these out with a vet visit before starting behavioral retraining.

**How long does it take to retrain an adult dog to go outside?**
Once any medical cause is ruled out, most adult dogs show a clear pattern within 2 to 3 weeks of a strict, consistent schedule, often faster than puppies since bladder control is already physically developed.

**Is it harder to house-train an adult dog than a puppy?**
Not inherently harder in terms of learning capacity, but an adult dog with an established indoor habit needs the scent fully removed from previous accident spots and a strict schedule to break the existing pattern, which can take focused management in the first couple of weeks.

**What's the difference between house-soiling and marking?**
House-soiling is a dog fully emptying its bladder indoors, usually a schedule or medical issue. Marking is typically small amounts deposited on vertical surfaces or specific objects, often triggered by new scents, stress, or intact hormonal status, and may need trigger-specific management alongside the standard retraining approach.

**Does diet change affect how often an adult dog needs to go outside?**
Yes, somewhat. A sudden food switch, increased water intake in hot weather, or new treats can shift elimination frequency temporarily, so factor recent diet changes into your schedule expectations rather than assuming a shift in frequency is purely behavioral.

**Will crate training work for an adult dog that never used one before?**
Most adult dogs can adapt to a crate with a gradual introduction, short sessions with high-value treats and meals fed inside, building duration over days rather than closing the door for a full night immediately. Dogs with prior negative crate experiences may need a slower reintroduction and, in some cases, respond better to a gated small room instead.

**Can neutering fix marking behavior?**
Neutering reduces marking behavior in many, but not all, intact male dogs, and effectiveness varies by how long the behavior has been established. It works best combined with the standard schedule-based retraining rather than as a standalone fix.

Multi-dog households add a layer of complexity worth naming directly: if one dog in the home is house-trained and another isn't, the untrained dog can pick up cues from the trained one, but the reverse also happens, a trained dog can start testing boundaries if it repeatedly witnesses another dog's accidents going unaddressed. Address the untrained dog's schedule directly rather than assuming proximity to a trained housemate will fix it on its own.

Keep expectations grounded in the individual dog's history: a dog with years of an established indoor pattern in a specific home may take longer to fully break the habit than one recently adopted into a brand-new environment with no prior indoor associations to overcome.

Bottom line: for adult dogs, rule out medical causes first, then apply the same strict schedule, immediate reward, and full management approach that works for puppies. Clean previous accident spots thoroughly with an enzymatic cleaner, and expect clear improvement within 2 to 3 weeks of consistent follow-through.

## Working around apartment living constraints

Adult dogs in apartments face a specific challenge: no yard means every outdoor trip requires a leash, an elevator or stairs, and often a longer walk to reach appropriate grass or ground. This delay between "needs to go" and "reaches the spot" can undermine an otherwise solid schedule. Build in extra buffer time on the schedule for apartment dogs, and consider a designated indoor turf box as a bridge option only if outdoor access is genuinely unpredictable, transitioning away from it once the outdoor schedule is reliably met.

## When to bring in a professional

If accidents continue past 4 to 6 weeks of a strict, consistent schedule with no medical cause found, especially in a household where the schedule truly was followed without gaps, it's worth consulting a certified professional trainer or, for cases with an anxiety component, a veterinary behaviorist. A fresh set of eyes often catches an inconsistency in the routine, an unsupervised gap, an unclear reward timing, that's easy to miss from inside the daily routine.
