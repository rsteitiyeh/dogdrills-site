The Dog Obedience Club of Lee County is an AKC-affiliated member club in Southwest Florida that runs group obedience classes from puppy basics through competition levels, hosts Canine Good Citizen evaluations, and sanctions AKC obedience trials. Group sessions typically run $100 to $180 for a six-to-eight week course. Annual membership costs $30 to $75. If you want a dog that holds a sit-stay while a toddler runs past, a county obedience club is one of the fastest ways to get there.

The value is not just the curriculum. It is the environment. You cannot proof a reliable recall in your backyard and expect it to hold at a crowded park. A club puts your dog in front of other dogs, strangers, smells, and noise every single week. That distraction is not incidental. It is the training.

## What does a dog obedience club of Lee County type actually offer?

AKC member clubs like those in Lee County run a full training ladder from puppy kindergarten through advanced competition obedience. You are not walking into a pet store class taught by a hired part-timer. Most club instructors compete with their own dogs and hold AKC obedience titles. Someone who has titled a dog through Utility knows every problem you are going to hit before you hit it.

A standard club calendar includes these tracks:

- Puppy socialization (8-16 weeks): name response, bite inhibition, basic leash manners, novel surface exposure
- Basic/Novice: sit, down, stay, come, loose-leash walking in a group setting
- Intermediate: off-leash basics, distance stays, out-of-sight stays, distraction proofing
- Canine Good Citizen prep: readiness for the AKC 10-skill behavioral evaluation
- Competition obedience: formal heeling, signal exercises, retrieves, scent discrimination, go-outs
- Open practice nights: unstructured floor time to proof behaviors with other dogs present

Practice nights are where gains actually consolidate. Show up to every one you can.

## How is club training different from hiring a private trainer?

The short answer: clubs train your dog around other dogs, and private trainers usually do not. That gap matters more than any other difference on the list.

| Format | Typical Cost | Distraction Level | Curriculum Depth |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dog obedience club (group) | $100-$180 per 6-8 week session | High (8-15 dogs) | Puppy through competition |
| Private trainer | $75-$150 per hour | Low to none | Usually basic only |
| Board-and-train | $1,200-$3,000 per 2-3 weeks | Variable | Variable |
| Pet store chain class | $120-$200 per 6-week session | Low (small groups) | Basic only |

The cost math favors clubs for most people. A six-week foundation course at a Lee County area club runs $100 to $180 total. Six private sessions at mid-range rates cost $450 to $900 for the same skills.

Private trainers are the better choice in two situations: dogs with active reactivity or bite history that make group settings unsafe, and handlers whose mechanics are not solid enough yet to get anything out of a class environment. Fix those things privately, then move into a club setting once the dog is stable. The formats work best together, not as replacements.

One thing clubs offer that private trainers cannot: an AKC-licensed evaluator on site. When you pass a CGC test at an affiliated club, the title goes into the AKC's national database under your dog's registration number. A private trainer's completion certificate lives in your glove box.

## What classes do AKC obedience clubs typically run?

| Level | Prerequisite | Core Skills | Duration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Puppy/Kindergarten | 8-16 weeks, vaccines current | Name, sit, manners, socialization | 4-6 weeks |
| Novice/Basic | 6 months+, basic attention | Heel, sit-stay, down-stay, recall | 6-8 weeks |

| Advanced/Open | Intermediate completion | Off-leash precision, drop on recall, trial prep | 8 weeks |
| Competition/Utility | Advanced completion | Scent articles, signals, directed jumping, go-outs | Ongoing |

Rally is worth flagging separately. The AKC added Rally as a titling sport in 2005, and most affiliated clubs now offer it because the entry curve is gentler than formal obedience. Handlers can talk to their dogs throughout the course. For owners who want a real training goal that stops short of full competition, Rally is the right on-ramp.

Not every club runs all six tracks. Call before assuming the curriculum matches what your dog needs right now.

## What should you look for when choosing a dog obedience club?

The AKC lists member clubs nationwide at [akc.org/sports/obedience](https://www.akc.org/sports/obedience/). For Lee County and surrounding Southwest Florida, that search pulls up local affiliates. Once you have candidates, evaluate them on these criteria:

**Instructor credentials.** Ask what titles the instructors hold on their own dogs. Companion Dog (CD), Companion Dog Excellent (CDX), Utility Dog (UD). If an instructor cannot tell you what their dogs have earned, that silence is informative. You want someone who has solved the problems you are about to face.

**Training methods.** Any club worth joining in 2026 teaches new behaviors through reward. Food and toys drive learning at every level. Some clubs add leash pressure and verbal corrections at advanced levels, and you can evaluate those mechanics once you see them. But if an instructor's default tool for teaching a new sit is a collar pop on a beginner dog, the curriculum is 20 years behind the research.

**Class size.** Eight to twelve dogs per instructor is functional. Over fifteen without assistants means you wait ten minutes between thirty-second working turns. Under four means you are paying group rates for something close to private without the individualized attention.

**Trial activity.** Does the club host AKC obedience trials? Clubs that run events stay engaged with the competitive community. That keeps their curriculum current and their instructors accountable.

**Facility conditions.** Rubber matting or sealed concrete matters for traction and footwork training. Southwest Florida humidity also matters. A club that trains outdoors in Lee County without a shade structure or heat plan in June is cutting corners on handler and dog safety.

**Observer policy.** Ask to watch a class before you register. Any reputable club says yes immediately. A club that discourages observers is telling you something.

## What does a dog obedience club session cost?

Costs split into two separate buckets that confuse first-time members: membership dues and class fees are billed separately.

Annual membership at most AKC-affiliated clubs runs $30 to $75 per individual. Dues cover AKC affiliation fees, liability insurance, and facility costs. Some clubs discount class rates for members; others charge the same to everyone.

Class fees by level in 2026:

- Puppy and novice group classes: $100 to $180 per 6-8 week session
- Intermediate and advanced levels: $120 to $220 per 6-8 week session
- Open practice nights: $5 to $15 drop-in per session
- CGC evaluation fee (AKC-administered): $20 per attempt

Coastal Southwest Florida sits at the higher end of these ranges compared to the national average. Lee County's cost of living pushes club fees above what a rural Midwest club charges for the same curriculum. Get the current fee schedule from the club directly before budgeting.

Annual membership typically pays for itself within the first month for handlers attending weekly classes and practice nights.

## What is AKC Canine Good Citizen certification and why do clubs offer it?

The Canine Good Citizen program is a ten-item behavioral evaluation developed by the AKC. It tests whether a dog can function in real public settings: accepting a friendly stranger, sitting politely for petting, walking loose-leash through a crowd, reacting calmly to another dog at close range, responding to basic commands, and holding a three-minute supervised separation while the handler is out of sight.

Passing earns an official AKC title logged in your dog's registration file. It also opens doors. Most therapy dog organizations, including Alliance of Therapy Dogs and Pet Partners, require CGC as the minimum baseline before a team can evaluate for placement. Many housing complexes with breed or size restrictions accept it as documented evidence of a well-managed dog.

The AKC has recorded over one million CGC completions since the program launched in 1989, making it the most widely recognized behavioral benchmark for pet dogs in the United States. Full test requirements are at [akc.org/products-services/training-programs/canine-good-citizen](https://www.akc.org/products-services/training-programs/canine-good-citizen/).

AKC-affiliated clubs can administer CGC evaluations on site. For members, that means testing on familiar ground after a regular class session, which removes a significant variable. A dog that has trained at the facility for weeks handles the test differently than one walking in cold.

## FAQ

**Can a mixed-breed dog participate in a dog obedience club of Lee County programs?**

Yes. Mixed-breed dogs can earn CGC titles and compete in all AKC performance sports including obedience, Rally, agility, and tracking through the AKC Canine Partners program. The only exclusion is conformation showing. Register a mixed-breed dog through the AKC Canine Partners enrollment before entering any AKC-licensed event through the club.

**What vaccinations are required before attending classes?**

Most AKC clubs require current rabies, DHPP (distemper, hepatitis, parvovirus, parainfluenza), and Bordetella before a dog enters the training building. Bring documentation to your first class. Some clubs also ask for a recent fecal screening. Confirm the full vaccine list with the specific club before showing up.

**Can I bring a reactive dog to group obedience classes?**

Not yet. Dogs with active leash reactivity toward other dogs or people belong in private behavioral work first, not group class. Putting a reactive dog into an uncontrolled group environment usually makes the reactivity worse. A good club instructor tells you this at intake and refers you to a behavioral specialist. Once the dog is stable enough to function near other dogs without triggering, the group setting becomes useful. That sequence matters.

**How long before a dog is trial-ready for AKC Novice obedience?**

Most teams need six to twelve months of consistent club training to be competitive in Novice class. The Novice requirements include heel on and off leash, figure-eights, sit-stay and down-stay with the handler out of sight, and a recall. Those skills take real time to proof to the precision the ring requires. Daily five-minute practice sessions at home accelerate the timeline more than extra class attendance does.

**What should I bring to the first class?**

A standard four-to-six foot flat leash (not retractable), high-value treats cut to pea size, a water bowl, and current vaccination records. Check the specific club's equipment policy before the first session. Some clubs have collar requirements that reflect their training methods. Knowing that before you arrive saves time and tells you something about how they work.
