A 28-day dog obedience challenge trains five core behaviors (sit, down, stay, recall, and loose-leash walking) using 10-minute sessions twice a day across four structured weeks. Advance each skill only when your dog hits an 80% success rate across three different environments. Most dogs with consistent owners complete all five behaviors on schedule.

The four-week window is a goal, not an ultimatum. Puppies under 16 weeks, dogs with fear histories, and breeds selected for independence often need six to eight weeks on the same material. What the structure gives you is a measurable framework to identify exactly which step in the chain needs more work.

## What skills does a 28-day dog obedience challenge cover?

The core five are sit, down, stay (with both duration and distance), recall (come when called), and loose-leash walking. These match the behaviors evaluated in the American Kennel Club's Canine Good Citizen (CGC) program, a widely recognized benchmark for basic obedience in the United States ([AKC CGC program overview](https://www.akc.org/products-services/training-programs/canine-good-citizen/)).

Two prerequisite skills come before the core five: name recognition and "leave it." Every subsequent command depends on name recognition. A dog that does not reliably turn toward its name in a distracting environment is not ready for recall training. "Leave it" builds the impulse control that makes week 3 and week 4 proofing possible. Both start on day one, before anything else.

Optional skills to add after week 2 if your dog is running ahead of schedule:

- **Place or mat stay**: a duration behavior where the dog holds a spot until released, which reinforces the stay work you have already done
- **Door manners**: no charging through exits before a release cue, which builds on stay and leave it simultaneously
- **Hand target (touch)**: the dog bumps its nose to your palm on cue, useful for dogs that struggle with the standard recall hand signal

Neither the place behavior nor door manners are required for the 28-day challenge. They are bonus work for dogs moving quickly through the material.

## How do you structure 28 days of dog training?

Four weeks, four focus areas. Each week builds directly on the last. Sessions stay at 10 minutes or less. Research on canine attention shows response rates decline in most dogs once sessions exceed 15 minutes, and the ASPCA's positive-reinforcement training guidance reflects this by recommending frequent, short sessions over long infrequent ones ([ASPCA dog training basics](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/dog-care/dog-training)).

| Week | Days | New Skills | Skills Under Review | Goal |
|------|------|------------|---------------------|------|
| Foundation | 1 to 7 | Name, sit, leave it | None | 80% on 10 consecutive trials |
| Core commands | 8 to 14 | Down, stay (2 sec / 2 ft) | Sit, name | 80% across 3 different locations |
| Distance and duration | 15 to 21 | Stay (10 sec / 10 ft), recall | All week 2 skills | 3 correct recalls in a row |
| Proofing | 22 to 28 | Loose-leash walking, door manners | All prior skills | Pass each skill in a public location |

"Proofing" means the dog performs reliably in the presence of real-world distractions. A sit in your kitchen and a sit in a park parking lot are two different behaviors from the dog's perspective. Most owners skip week 4 proofing and then wonder why their dog ignores them at the dog park. Do not skip it. Week 4 is the difference between a trained dog and a dog that performs tricks at home.

## What if your dog keeps failing the same skill?

Drop back one step and find exactly where the behavior falls apart. This is not a regression. This is the correct training move.

The ASPCA's approach to positive reinforcement starts from this principle: break any behavior into the smallest possible steps, reward each step separately, and chain them only after each component is solid. If your dog cannot hold a two-second stay, the difficulty level is simply wrong right now. Drop to a one-second stay, get 10 consecutive successes, and then add one second at a time.

Three failure patterns that come up most often in a 28-day challenge:

**Stay breaks the moment you move.** You added distance before the dog had duration. Back off to duration-only stays while standing directly next to your dog. Build to 30 seconds at zero feet before you take a single step away.

**Sit does not generalize beyond the kitchen.** You trained sit in one place, so the dog learned a kitchen sit, not a sit. Train in five different spots before the end of week 1, including outside on the sidewalk.

**Recall fails around any distraction.** The distraction level exceeds the dog's current training level. Practice recall in your backyard before taking it to a park. Distractions are not obstacles to avoid; they are criteria to add gradually, one level at a time.

Log every session. Write the skill, the success rate as a percentage, and any notable trigger or context. Three sessions of logs will show you exactly where the chain breaks. Patterns in training logs are the fastest diagnostic tool you have.

## Does 28 days actually work for all dogs?

It works reliably for adult dogs (18 months and older) when the owner trains daily and advances only on the 80% threshold. It does not work identically for all ages or all breeds.

Puppies under 16 weeks need 3-5 minute sessions, fewer consecutive repetitions per skill, and adjusted expectations. A 10-week-old puppy cannot hold a 10-second stay. The prefrontal cortex development required for that kind of impulse control is not complete. At that age, work on name recognition, basic sit, and short attention-building games.

Breed influences the timeline more than most owners expect. Retrievers, herding breeds, and working dogs often complete the core five skills in two to three weeks because they were selectively bred to take direction from humans. Scent hounds and Nordic breeds (huskies, malamutes, basenjis) commonly need the recall work extended by one to two weeks because those breeds were developed for independent decision-making rather than responsiveness to human cuing.

Mixed-breed dogs generally track the dominant breed type. A lab-husky mix tends to breeze through foundation and core commands, then hit a wall on week 3 recall. Knowing the likely sticking point ahead of time prevents frustration and helps you allocate your reps in week 2 toward that behavior before it becomes a problem.

## How long should each training session be?

Ten minutes maximum, twice a day. Morning and evening sessions with at least four hours between them produce better skill retention than a single 20-minute block. Dogs consolidate what they practiced between sessions. The gap between sessions is not wasted time.

The 10-minute cap forces you to end on a success. Sessions that run long almost always end with a failed attempt, because the dog has habituated to the repetition and attention has dropped. That failed attempt is the last thing the dog remembers about training that day. When the timer goes off, ask for one easy behavior the dog knows cold, reward it well, and stop.

Use a physical kitchen timer, not a mental one. Most people's sense of 10 minutes stretches when training is going well and compresses when it is not. Set the timer. When it goes off, end the session regardless of where you are in the skill sequence.

Two sessions per day is the minimum for the 28-day timeline. One session a day slows progress to roughly six weeks on the same material. If twice a day is not possible on a given day, one session still counts. Missing an entire day occasionally is not a problem. Missing three or more consecutive days in week 3 typically means repeating week 3 before moving to proofing.

## What equipment do you need for the challenge?

Three things: a 6-foot leash, a flat collar or front-clip harness, and high-value treats. High value means the dog will work for it in a parking lot with other dogs nearby. Cooked chicken cut into pieces smaller than your pinky fingernail, string cheese, or commercial liver treats cover this for most dogs. Kibble works for easy skills in a quiet room. It fails in almost every week 4 proofing scenario.

For week 4, add a 15 to 30-foot long line. The long line gives you real distance for recall practice without the dog leaving the training area. It is the most useful tool for proofing recall that most owners never buy. Do not substitute a retractable leash. The variable tension from a retractable leash teaches the dog to pull against pressure, which directly undermines loose-leash walking work.

A clicker is optional but efficient for marking precise behaviors like the exact moment the dog's rear hits the ground on a sit. If you use one, your timing must land within half a second of the behavior. A late click marks the wrong behavior every time. If you are uncertain about your timing, use a verbal marker ("yes!") instead. A consistent verbal marker works as well as a clicker when the timing is reliable.

## FAQ

**How many minutes a day does the 28-day dog obedience challenge take?**

Twenty minutes total: two 10-minute sessions with at least four hours between them. On days when time is short, one session still counts toward progress. Missing one day occasionally is not a problem. Missing three or more consecutive days in week 3 usually means running week 3 again before advancing to proofing.

**Can an older dog do a 28-day obedience challenge?**

Yes, and adult dogs often train faster than puppies because they have longer attention spans and more natural impulse control. The main adjustment for senior dogs (over 8 years in large breeds, over 10 in small breeds) is watching for joint discomfort on the down command. If the dog hesitates before lying down or favors a leg, swap to a hand-target behavior or work on softer surfaces like grass or a yoga mat.

**What treats work best during the 28-day challenge?**

Small, soft, and smelly. Pieces smaller than your pinky fingernail keep the dog motivated across a 10-minute session without filling up. For week 4 proofing in high-distraction locations, introduce a treat the dog has never received before. Novel food carries higher reinforcement value than familiar food when competing against distractions like other dogs, squirrels, or moving vehicles.

**Does my dog need to be in a class to do the 28-day challenge?**

No, but a group class running concurrently is useful for week 4 proofing. Working around other dogs provides built-in distractions without you having to engineer them. A four-week beginner obedience class that starts when you start the challenge works well in parallel because it covers the same core skills at a slightly slower pace, giving you reinforcement on both tracks.

**What comes after day 28?**

Maintenance. Ask for the five core behaviors throughout daily life rather than in formal sessions. Sit before meals, stay before the door opens, recall before every off-leash moment. Behaviors that stop getting practiced in real contexts decay over months. The 28 days build the behaviors. The rest of the dog's life maintains them.

## Bottom line

The 28-day dog obedience challenge works when you train daily, advance only on the 80% threshold, and complete week 4 proofing in real locations with real distractions. Dogs that need more than 28 days are not failing the challenge. They are showing you which step in the chain needs more repetitions before it holds in the real world. Find that step, fix it, and move on. The structure holds.
