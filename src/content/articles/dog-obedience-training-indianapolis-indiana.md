Dog obedience training in Indianapolis, Indiana covers sit, stay, come, loose-leash walking, and impulse control through group classes or private sessions. Group programs run $100-$250 total over 6-8 weeks. Private sessions cost $75-$150 per hour. Results depend on 10-15 minutes of daily practice at home, not just showing up to class once a week.

Indiana does not license dog trainers. Anyone can call themselves one. That puts the screening burden on you before you pay.

## What does dog obedience training cover?

A proper obedience program builds six core skills: sit, down, stay, recall (come when called), heel or loose-leash walking, and leave it. Those six form the floor for every other behavior. Without a reliable recall, off-leash play is a liability. Without a solid stay, answering the front door safely is a gamble every time.

Most reputable programs also include handling exercises: tolerance for ear, paw, and body contact. This matters because a dog that shuts down at the vet is a dog you cannot treat. A lot of budget group classes skip handling. Ask specifically whether it is in the curriculum before you enroll.

Advanced classes build on the foundation with distance, duration, and distraction work. That is the real test. A dog that sits reliably in your quiet kitchen is not a trained dog. A dog that holds a sit at a busy sidewalk cafe while a kid runs past is. Push for that standard and your trainer should be pushing you toward it.

## What types of training programs are available in Indianapolis?

Indianapolis has four main formats, each suited to a different situation.

**Group classes** run 6-8 weeks at one hour per session alongside five to eight other dogs. The social reps are built in, which is a real advantage for puppies and undersocialized adults. This is the most cost-effective format for dogs without serious behavior issues.

**Private sessions** give you one trainer, one dog, one problem. In-home privates let the trainer see exactly what is happening in your actual environment, which reveals things a facility setting never shows. Go private for leash reactivity, aggression toward guests, or any behavior that a group setting would make worse before it gets better.

**Board-and-train** sends your dog to the trainer's facility for two to four weeks. Dogs get 5-8 training repetitions per day instead of one 15-minute home session. Fastest skill acquisition, highest cost, and it requires the most careful vetting because you are not there to watch daily.

**Day training** is a hybrid: the trainer works with your dog during the day and you practice in the evenings with coaching on what to do. You stay involved without blocking off weekends.

## How do you pick a qualified trainer in Indiana?

Indiana has no state licensing requirement for dog trainers, which makes voluntary credentials meaningful when a trainer earns them.

**CPDT-KA** (Certified Professional Dog Trainer, Knowledge Assessed) from the Certification Council for Professional Dog Trainers requires 300 hours of documented experience and a proctored exam. It is the most common baseline credential in the field. The [Association of Professional Dog Trainers](https://apdt.com/resource-center/finding-a-trainer/) keeps a searchable directory of members with verified credentials, which is a reasonable starting point for finding someone in the Indianapolis area.

Karen Pryor Academy graduates (KPA-CTP) and IAABC-certified consultants are also solid. For cases involving aggression or serious anxiety, look specifically for IAABC-ADT or CDBC certification, which requires demonstrated competency with behavior cases, not just obedience work.

Steer clear of trainers who rely primarily on prong collars, shock collars, or physical corrections as their main tools. Reward-based methods produce stronger long-term skill retention and fewer fallout behaviors like avoidance and redirected aggression. This is not a philosophical position; it is what the peer-reviewed behavioral science on learning consistently shows.

Before you pay anyone, ask for a facility tour and to observe one class. Any reputable trainer will say yes immediately. Ask what they do when a dog refuses to respond to a cue. That answer tells you exactly how they work. If the answer involves a correction before the dog has been given enough repetitions to understand the cue, look elsewhere.

Red flags that should end the conversation: no credential you can verify, refusal to let you watch a class, claims to have a "secret" method, or a guarantee of results without meeting your dog first.

## How long does it take to see results from obedience training?

Most dogs show clear, measurable improvement within 2-4 weeks of consistent training. Consistent means 10-15 minutes of practice every single day at home, not just the weekly class.

Puppies under 6 months are in a critical socialization window that closes around 12-14 weeks of age. Start at 8 weeks. The old advice to wait until 6 months before training wastes the single most important developmental period your dog will ever have. Early classes do not overwhelm puppies. They build confidence exactly when new experiences shape behavior most deeply.

Adult dogs learn new skills without much trouble. Replacing existing habits takes a bit longer than building new ones from scratch, but the process is the same. A 7-year-old dog with zero training background can develop a reliable sit, stay, and recall in 4-6 weeks of daily work.

The AKC's Canine Good Citizen (CGC) program gives you a concrete, objective benchmark to train toward. Dogs must pass 10 tests: accepting a friendly stranger, sitting politely for petting, tolerating grooming and handling, walking on a loose leash, walking through a crowd, sitting and staying on command, coming when called, reacting appropriately to another dog, handling distractions without falling apart, and tolerating supervised separation. The [full CGC test criteria and evaluator directory are on the AKC website](https://www.akc.org/products-services/training-programs/canine-good-citizen/). Most dogs can pass after a solid 8-week class combined with consistent daily practice.

## Is board-and-train worth the cost?

Board-and-train delivers results. Whether those results stick depends entirely on what happens after pickup.

Dogs trained by a skilled professional 5-8 times per day for three weeks will look dramatically different when they come home. That is real and it happens consistently with quality programs. What is also real: those skills were built in the trainer's environment, with the trainer's cues and timing. Put a different person on the other end of the leash and the behavior often degrades within days.

A reputable board-and-train includes 2-3 handler transfer sessions before the dog goes home, where you learn to cue the same behaviors with the same timing. Ask about this before you sign anything. If the trainer says the dog will "just know what to do" when it gets home, that is not a $2,000 program worth paying for.

Board-and-train makes the most sense in three situations: owners with genuinely limited time for daily training, dogs with complex problem behaviors that need intensive work before group class is feasible, and situations where a dog needs foundational skills fast (a new rescue before a household addition, for example).

For basic obedience on a straightforward dog, a group class plus daily home practice gets you to the same place for a fraction of the cost.

## FAQ

**How old does my dog need to be to start obedience training in Indianapolis?**

Eight weeks. Puppy classes for dogs 8-16 weeks old focus on socialization, bite inhibition, and foundational skills like sit and come. The old standard of waiting until 6 months is outdated guidance that wastes your dog's most developmentally sensitive period. Starting at 8 weeks does not confuse puppies; it shapes behavior during the window when shaping behavior is easiest.

**Does Indianapolis require dogs to complete obedience training?**

No, not as a general requirement. However, dogs with a documented bite history may fall under Indiana's dangerous dog statute (Indiana Code 15-20-1), which can mandate a behavioral evaluation and training as conditions for keeping the dog. If your dog has bitten someone, contact Indianapolis Animal Care Services before the situation becomes a municipal enforcement issue.

**Can an adult or senior dog learn basic obedience?**

Yes, without qualification. The "old dogs can't learn" idea is wrong. Adult dogs learn at roughly the same rate as young dogs; they just need a bit more repetition to replace existing habits. A 9-year-old dog that has never had any training can develop a reliable sit, stay, and recall in 4-6 weeks of daily work. Senior dogs often do better with shorter, more frequent sessions rather than longer ones, since physical comfort matters more as they age.

**What treats work best in an obedience class?**

High-value treats: small pieces of real chicken, cheese, or hot dog. Not kibble. In a group class environment you are competing with every distraction in the room. High-value means your dog would stop sniffing another dog to eat it. Keep pieces tiny (pea-sized) so your dog does not fill up after 12 repetitions. If your dog refuses food in class, that is usually stress, not pickiness. Talk to your trainer about it rather than pushing through.

**What is the difference between a dog trainer and a veterinary behaviorist?**

A dog trainer teaches skills and modifies everyday behavior problems. A veterinary behaviorist (DACVB) holds a veterinary degree plus a full residency in behavioral medicine and is the right referral for clinical-level problems: fear aggression severe enough to require medication, separation anxiety causing self-injury, compulsive behaviors. If your dog's issues go beyond obedience, start with your vet and ask for a referral. A group class is not the right tool for a clinical anxiety disorder.
