Obedience training is not about a dog performing tricks on command in your living room. It's about a dog that listens when there's a squirrel, a dropped pill bottle, or a stranger at the door. The direct answer: five commands cover almost every real-world obedience need, sit, down, stay, come, and leave it, and you build each one with three to five short sessions a day of five to ten reps, moving from a quiet room to real distractions over two to four weeks per command.

## Why most obedience training stalls out

Owners commonly teach a dog to sit in the kitchen, declare success, then wonder why the dog ignores "sit" at the dog park. The command was never generalized past one room. The American Kennel Club's training guidance repeatedly emphasizes practicing commands in a range of locations and distraction levels as the actual work of obedience training, not the initial teaching (akc.org). Below, every drill includes a generalization step for exactly this reason.

## Drill 1: Sit

Hold a treat at your dog's nose, then move it up and slightly back over the head. Most dogs' rear ends drop into a sit as they follow the treat up. The instant the sit happens, mark with "yes" and treat. Add the word "sit" only after the behavior is reliable on the lure, saying it right as the dog's rear touches the ground, not before.

- **Session 1-3**: lure only, no verbal cue, 8 reps per session, kitchen or living room
- **Session 4-6**: add verbal "sit" cue as the dog starts moving into position
- **Session 7-10**: fade the treat lure to an empty hand gesture, then to no gesture
- **Generalization**: repeat the whole sequence in the backyard, then on a walk, then near mild distractions like another person

Most dogs reach a reliable indoor "sit" within a week and a distraction-proof one within three to four weeks.

## Drill 2: Down

Down builds off sit. Start with your dog sitting, hold a treat at nose level, then lower it straight to the floor between the front paws. Most dogs follow the treat down into a lying position. If your dog's rear pops up instead of the front end lowering, you're moving the treat too fast or too far from the body; slow down and keep the treat glued to the floor.

Rep count mirrors sit: 8 reps per session, 2 to 3 sessions a day, verbal cue added once the lure works reliably. Down tends to take slightly longer than sit for larger or stiffer-jointed dogs, so budget an extra week for breeds like Mastiffs or senior dogs.

## Drill 3: Stay

Stay is a duration behavior, and the most common training error is adding distance before duration or distraction is solid. Build it in this order:

1. Cue sit or down, say "stay," count one second, then release with a distinct word like "okay" and treat.
2. Extend duration in small increments: 2 seconds, 5, 10, 20, 30, always releasing before the dog breaks position on its own.
3. Once duration hits 30 seconds reliably, add distance: one step back, return, release, treat. Build distance the same incremental way.
4. Only once both duration and distance are solid do you add distraction: a dropped toy, another person walking by, a doorbell sound.

| Stage | Target before advancing |
|---|---|
| Duration | 30 seconds, no distance, no distraction |
| Distance | 10 feet, low distraction |
| Distraction | Holds stay with a bounced ball or knock at the door |

Rushing straight to "stay while I answer the front door" in week one is the number one reason stay training falls apart.

## Drill 4: Come (recall)

Recall is the highest-stakes command you'll teach because a dog that won't come back is a dog that can run into a road. Start indoors with zero distractions. Say your dog's name plus "come" in an upbeat voice, and the instant the dog looks toward you or moves your way, mark and reward heavily, better treats than any other drill gets. Never call your dog to come for something unpleasant, a bath, nail trims, being put away, because that pairs the word with punishment fast.

Build recall distance gradually using a long training line (15 to 30 feet) outdoors before ever trusting it off-leash in an open space. Practice recall away from mild distractions first (a person standing still), then moving distractions (someone walking, tossing a ball), before testing it around genuinely exciting triggers like other dogs.

## Drill 5: Leave it

Leave it stops your dog from grabbing dropped food, trash, or something dangerous. Start with a treat closed in your fist. Let your dog sniff and paw at the fist; the instant they back off or stop trying, say "leave it," mark, and reward from your OTHER hand, never the fist they just left alone. This teaches the dog that ignoring the tempting item earns a better reward elsewhere.

Progress to a treat on the floor covered by your hand, then an uncovered treat you can cover quickly if the dog lunges, then finally a treat on the floor with no hand covering it at all, cueing "leave it" verbally before the dog reaches it.

## Building a daily obedience routine

Five to ten minutes, three times a day, beats one 30-minute session for almost every dog, because attention span and food motivation both drop off fast in longer sessions. A realistic weekly split for the average adult dog looks like: mornings for sit and down refreshers, midday for stay duration work, evenings for recall or leave it in the yard. Rotate which command gets the main focus each week rather than drilling all five daily at full intensity, which burns out both of you.

## Troubleshooting stalled commands

If a command that worked yesterday suddenly falls apart, the usual causes are: you increased difficulty too fast (added distraction before duration was solid), the reward value dropped (using kibble instead of high-value treats in a distracting environment), or the dog is tired, overstimulated, or not feeling well. Veterinary behavior professionals note that sudden, unexplained drops in trained behavior sometimes flag pain or discomfort worth a vet check, particularly in older dogs (dacvb.org).

## Adding real-world proofing to each command

A command that only works in a quiet kitchen isn't finished training, it's finished with step one. Proofing means deliberately testing each command under conditions that resemble where you'll actually need it: a sit at a busy trailhead, a down-stay while a delivery truck idles outside, a recall at the dog park with other dogs mid-play. Introduce one new variable at a time, location, distraction, or duration, rather than stacking several new challenges into a single session. If your dog fails a proofing test, that's useful information, not a setback: drop back one level of difficulty and rebuild before trying the harder version again.

## Working with multiple dogs in the same household

Training two or more dogs together multiplies distraction for both of them, since each dog reacts to the other's excitement and movement. It's usually faster to teach each command to each dog separately first, then practice together once both dogs have an individually solid foundation. When training together, use each dog's name clearly before any cue so neither dog is guessing whether the command applies to them, and reward the dog that responds correctly even if the other dog also reacted, to avoid accidentally reinforcing a "wait and copy" pattern in the slower learner.

## Matching training intensity to age and breed

Puppies under six months have shorter attention spans and lower physical stamina for training sessions, so keep sessions brief, three to five minutes, several times a day, rather than one long block. Adult dogs generally handle longer sessions, up to ten minutes, without losing focus. Working and herding breeds often want more mental repetition and progress toward advanced variations faster; toy and companion breeds can master the same core commands just as reliably but sometimes need extra patience with distraction-proofing due to differing motivation levels around food and toys.

## Keeping training sessions fun for both of you

Obedience training that feels like a chore for the handler tends to produce inconsistent results, since flat, unenthusiastic delivery of cues and rewards reads to the dog as low-value. Keep your tone upbeat, celebrate small wins out loud, and end each session on a successful rep rather than pushing until a mistake happens. A dog that associates training time with genuine enjoyment engages faster and retains commands longer than one trained through repetition alone.

## FAQ

**How long does basic obedience training take?**
Most dogs learn the five core commands to a reliable indoor standard within 4 to 6 weeks of daily short sessions. Full distraction-proofing, the kind that holds up at a busy park, realistically takes 3 to 6 months of ongoing practice.

**Is it too late to obedience train an adult dog?**
No. Adult dogs often learn faster than puppies because they have longer attention spans, though breaking existing bad habits (like jumping) can take longer than teaching a fresh behavior from scratch.

**Do I need treats forever?**
No, but fade them gradually rather than abruptly. Move from a treat every rep to every other rep, then random reps, then intermittent real-world rewards like praise or a favorite toy, over several weeks per command.

**What if my dog only listens when I have food in my hand?**
That means the treat became a bribe instead of a reward. Practice showing an empty hand before cuing the command, and only reveal the treat after the dog complies, not before.

**Should I use a clicker for obedience commands?**
A clicker can sharpen your timing since it marks the exact right moment more precisely than a spoken word, but it's optional. Consistent, well-timed verbal marking works fine for basic obedience.

Pick one command to focus on this week, run the reps daily, and resist the urge to move to distraction training before duration and distance are locked in at home.
