Training dog puppy work starts at 8 weeks with 3-to-5-minute reward-based sessions run three to five times daily. Teach sit, name recognition, and come in that order. Most puppies hold their first five commands reliably by 12 to 16 weeks when owners train every single day. Starting after 14 weeks works but costs you the easiest learning window a dog will ever have.

How hard puppy training gets depends on three variables: starting age, household consistency, and whether socialization runs alongside obedience on the same schedule. Nail all three and most breeds show solid basic obedience by 16 weeks. Miss any one and you spend the next year correcting habits instead of building them.

## How do you start training a dog puppy at 8 weeks?

Start simple: pick a small quiet room, cut treats into pea-sized pieces, and run your first session the day the puppy comes home.

The first session lasts three minutes. You are not teaching obedience yet. You are teaching the puppy that paying attention to you produces food. Hold a treat near your nose so the puppy looks at your eyes. The instant eye contact happens, mark it with "yes" or a click and deliver the treat. Do that five times and end. That is session one.

On day two, add name conditioning. Say the puppy's name once at a conversational volume. When the puppy looks at you, mark and reward. Never repeat the name three times in a row and reward on the third. That teaches the puppy that the first call is optional. One name, one look, one reward.

The AKC identifies 8 to 10 weeks as the prime window for foundation work and notes that puppies who start in this window build cleaner command responses than puppies who start at 3 or 4 months ([AKC Puppy Training Timeline](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/training/puppy-training-timeline/)). The window does not slam shut at 14 weeks, but effort multiplies after that point.

Keep the location consistent for the first two weeks, then start moving sessions to new rooms. Puppies learn behaviors tied to context before they generalize them. A puppy who sits reliably in the kitchen may have no idea what "sit" means in the backyard the first time you ask there.

## What commands should you teach when training dog puppies?

Teach in this order: sit, name recall, down, stay, come. That sequence is not arbitrary.

Sit is the easiest because a puppy naturally shifts its rear down when a treat moves backward over its head. You can get a sit in the first session. Once sit is solid at 8 out of 10 responses on the first ask, move to name recall. Name recall builds the habit of orienting to you, which sets up every other command you will ever teach.

Down follows sit because it requires the puppy to lower its whole body. Some puppies find that posture vulnerable and resist early in training. If you attempt down before sit is solid, you fight two battles at once.

Stay and come come last because they require impulse control the puppy has not built yet. A 9-week-old puppy cannot hold still for more than a second or two. Train stay in 1-second increments and add duration one second per day rather than one second per session. Rush this and you get a puppy that breaks its stay constantly because you extended duration faster than the dog's self-control developed.

Skip tricks like shake or spin until the five core commands are solid. Tricks feel like progress but they do not build the impulse control you need when real-world distractions appear at 5 or 6 months.

## What training methods work best for puppy training?

Luring, capturing, and shaping are the three core methods. Use all three, each for the right situation.

Luring means guiding the puppy's body into position with a treat. It is the fastest way to get a new behavior started. The risk: puppies trained only with lures often stop responding when the treat disappears from the hand. Fade the lure within 3 to 5 repetitions by switching to an empty hand with the same motion, then deliver the treat from your pocket.

Capturing means marking a behavior the puppy does naturally. The puppy lies down on its own: say "yes" and reward. You have now started building "down" without a lure. Capturing produces durable responses because the puppy chose the behavior. It is slower to start but locks in faster once the puppy understands the game.

Shaping means rewarding steps toward a final behavior. Want the puppy to place its chin on the floor? Start by rewarding any downward head movement, then only lower movements, then chin at 6 inches from the floor, then chin touching. Shaping builds problem-solving and exhausts puppies mentally faster than luring, useful for high-drive breeds that need more than physical exercise to settle.

Avoid physical corrections, leash pops, or any punishing tool in the first months. The ASPCA position on reward-based training with young dogs is consistent: positive reinforcement produces faster initial learning and fewer behavioral side effects than punishment-based approaches ([ASPCA Dog Behavior Resources](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/dog-care/dog-behavior-problems)). Punishment raises arousal. Raised arousal makes learning harder, not easier.

## How does socialization fit into training dog puppy schedules?

Socialization is training. Put it in the same daily time block, not as a separate project you get to eventually.

Between 8 and 14 weeks, a puppy's brain runs a sensitivity sweep. Every novel experience during this window gets categorized as normal. Everything the puppy does not encounter gets categorized as potentially threatening. This is not a metaphor: it reflects how canine neurological development works during the critical socialization period.

The practical target: the puppy should meet 100 new things in the first 100 days. Veterinary behaviorists who tracked outcome data across large numbers of dogs have consistently pointed to this level of exposure as correlated with fewer adult fear-based behavioral problems. Those "things" include surfaces (concrete, grass, gravel, carpet, tile, metal grating), sounds (traffic, children, vacuum cleaners, thunder recordings), people of different heights and appearances, and other animals seen at a safe distance.

Structure every exposure: pair each new thing with a treat. A stranger approaches and the puppy gets a treat. A loud truck drives by and the puppy gets a treat. A slippery floor appears and you scatter treats on it. The puppy is not learning "slippery floors are fun." The puppy is learning "novel things near my person are manageable."

One thing to watch: socialization does not mean flooding. Do not carry a frightened puppy toward something that is scaring it. Let the puppy set the pace and approach voluntarily. Forcing contact with a fear-inducing stimulus at 9 weeks can create avoidance and fear responses that last for years and require professional intervention to address.

## What does a puppy training schedule look like by age?

Use this table as your starting roadmap. Adjust up if your puppy is ahead; hold at a stage if the dog needs more time before moving forward.

| Age | Daily Sessions | Session Length | Main Focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8 to 10 weeks | 4 to 5 | 3 min each | Name, sit, eye contact, body handling, socialization |
| 10 to 12 weeks | 4 | 3 to 5 min | Recall, leash introduction, collar conditioning |
| 12 to 16 weeks | 3 to 4 | 5 min | Down, stay (1 to 5 seconds), door manners, location changes |
| 4 to 6 months | 3 | 5 to 10 min | Stay with distance, loose-leash walking, leave it |
| 6 to 12 months | 2 | 10 to 15 min | Distraction proofing, real-world generalization |

Three rules for this schedule: if the puppy masters a skill faster, move on the same day. If the puppy regresses after a week of solid performance, drop back one step and rebuild rather than pushing forward. If the puppy refuses food during a session, stop: the puppy is either sick, overstimulated, or too tired to learn, and pushing through teaches nothing and builds a negative association with training.

## Why does puppy training seem to fall apart at 4 to 6 months?

It is not broken. It is adolescence, and it is predictable.

At roughly 4 to 6 months, most puppies hit a phase where behaviors that were solid become inconsistent. The puppy who recalled reliably for weeks suddenly seems selective about it. The dog who held a stay now breaks it after two seconds. Owners who do not expect this conclude the training did not work and reduce or stop training entirely.

What is actually happening: the brain is reorganizing. Hormonal shifts, a second fear period that typically appears between 8 and 10 months, and a general surge in environmental awareness all compete with trained behaviors simultaneously. This phase is temporary and trainable through.

Owners who keep working with the same methods during adolescence come out the other side with solid adult dogs. Owners who stop training during this phase teach the dog that commands are situational suggestions rather than reliable behaviors.

The mechanical fix: shorten sessions, raise reward value, lower criteria temporarily (if the dog was holding 30-second stays, drop to 10), and add more repetitions per session. The regression window is usually 4 to 8 weeks. Keep working.

## What treats work best for training dog puppies?

Size matters more than type. Keep treats to pea-sized pieces: small enough to consume in under two seconds, large enough to register as a reward.

Use three tiers based on difficulty. Kibble works for known behaviors in low-distraction environments. Mid-value treats (commercial training treats, plain cooked chicken pieces) work for new behaviors and moderate distraction. High-value treats (small cheddar cubes, hot dog pieces, plain cooked liver) are for recall and for training in high-distraction environments like parks or around other dogs.

The calorie limit: training treats should not exceed 10% of the puppy's daily calorie intake. A 10-pound puppy eating 400 calories of kibble per day can take in 40 training-treat calories before risking weight gain or digestive upset. That translates to roughly 80 pea-sized chicken pieces per day, which is more than enough for five sessions with leftover room. Subtract training treat calories from the daily kibble amount by pulling out a small handful of kibble from the morning meal before each training day. No precision math required.

## Frequently asked questions about training dog puppies

**At what age should you start training dog puppies?**

Start at 8 weeks, the day the puppy comes home. Do not wait for vaccinations to complete, for the puppy to settle in for a month, or for a class slot to open. Three-minute sessions with food rewards work immediately. The 8-to-14-week window is when new experiences transfer into long-term memory most efficiently, the best window for foundational learning.

**How long does training a dog puppy take?**

Basic commands (sit, down, stay, come, polite leash walking) are solid in most puppies by 16 to 20 weeks with consistent daily training. Full reliability in distracting environments takes another 4 to 6 months of deliberate proofing. A puppy is not truly trained until it performs commands outdoors near other dogs and strangers under real-world conditions. Kitchen-only obedience is a starting point, not the finish line.

**Does puppy training require a professional trainer?**

No, but a good puppy class adds two things that are hard to replicate alone: controlled socialization with other puppies, and an instructor watching your mechanics in real time. If your timing is off when you mark a behavior, that is nearly impossible to self-diagnose from inside the training moment. One 6-week group class corrects timing errors faster than months of solo work. Most facilities run puppy group classes for $100 to $200 for a 6-week session.

**What if a puppy refuses food during training sessions?**

Try these in order: train before meals rather than after (a full puppy has less motivation), raise the treat value by switching from kibble to chicken or cheese, and shorten the session to 90 seconds. Truly food-unmotivated puppies are rare. In most cases the puppy is satiated, the reward is not competitive with the current distraction level, or the session has run too long and the puppy has mentally checked out.

**Is training a dog puppy different from training an adult dog?**

The methods are identical. What changes is session length and starting complexity. An adult dog works productively for 15 to 20 minutes on complex behaviors. A puppy needs 3 to 5 minutes on simple ones. Adult dogs that missed early training take longer to build new habits because competing patterns are already established, but they learn. Reward-based training works for dogs at every age.

Bottom line: training dog puppy success runs on three things: starting at 8 weeks, keeping sessions short enough to end before the puppy loses focus, and running socialization in parallel with obedience every single week. Follow the schedule in this guide, hold every household member to the same rules, and the payoff is a dog that behaves reliably at 12 months rather than one you are still managing at 3 years.
