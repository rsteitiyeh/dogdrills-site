Tri state dog obedience clubs are regional training organizations that prepare dogs and handlers for AKC competition. They run group classes, practice matches, and licensed trials across a cluster of neighboring states. To earn any AKC obedience title, a dog needs three qualifying scores (170 or higher out of 200 points) under at least two different judges. The exercises, scoring rules, and title requirements are identical everywhere; what a tri-state club network adds is trial density and instructor depth.

If you are searching for a tri state dog obedience program or club near a multi-state metro area, here is exactly what those clubs teach, what each competition level requires, and how to find a legitimate program.

## What does a dog obedience club teach?

A club teaches handlers and dogs to perform a precise sequence of exercises on cue, under distraction, in front of a judge. This is not pet manners training. It is a sport with a fixed point system, specific exercises at each level, and clear pass/fail criteria per exercise.

The foundation is always the same: the dog heels at your left side at your exact walking pace, sits automatically when you stop, holds a stay while you walk across the ring, and returns to you on a single recall command. Off leash. With other dogs nearby.

Group classes at an obedience club serve two purposes the average pet training class cannot replicate: your dog learns to work around other dogs at competition distance, and your instructor teaches the precision that trial judges score. A solid sit 8 inches from your heel is not the same sit as the one your dog does in your kitchen. Club training builds the competition-ready version.

Most clubs also run the AKC Canine Good Citizen (CGC) program as a prerequisite or beginner stepping stone. The CGC evaluates 10 behaviors including polite greeting, walking through a crowd, and a three-minute down-stay out of sight. According to the AKC, the program has been in place since 1989 and is recognized by a number of housing organizations and therapy-dog certification bodies ([AKC CGC program](https://www.akc.org/products-services/training-programs/canine-good-citizen/)). If your dog cannot pass CGC, it is not ready for trial prep.

## What are the AKC obedience levels and what does each require?

The AKC obedience program has four title levels. Each adds difficulty and demands more independence from the dog. Here is what each level covers:

| Level | AKC Title | Key Exercises | Max Points | Qualifying Score |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Novice | CD (Companion Dog) | Heel on leash, heel off leash, figure eight, stand for exam by stranger, recall, long sit (1 min), long down (3 min) | 200 | 170+ with no zero on any exercise |
| Open | CDX (Companion Dog Excellent) | Drop on recall, retrieve on flat, retrieve over high jump, broad jump, heel free, long sit/down out of handler sight | 200 | 170+ with no zero on any exercise |
| Utility | UD (Utility Dog) | Signal exercise (all commands by hand signal only), scent discrimination on two articles, directed retrieve, moving stand and exam, directed jumping | 200 | 170+ with no zero on any exercise |
| Utility Excellent | UDX | Qualify in both Open B and Utility B at the same licensed trial on the same day, 10 times | N/A | 10 double-qualify scores |

The zero rule is what catches most handlers off guard the first time. A dog can earn 168 points on nine perfect exercises and NQ (non-qualify) because it broke its long down on the tenth. There is no partial credit for a dropped article or a missed signal. That binary rule is why club training focuses on proofing the weak exercises, not polishing the strong ones.

The full AKC obedience regulations, including the specific point deductions for each exercise, are published at [akc.org/sports/obedience](https://www.akc.org/sports/obedience/). Serious competitors read it before their first trial.

## How much does dog obedience club training cost?

Class fees at club-affiliated programs run $100 to $250 for a 6-to-8-week session. Trial entry fees are $25 to $35 per class per trial day (Novice A, Novice B, Open A, and so on are each a separate entry). Annual club membership usually runs $30 to $75 and covers access to training nights and club-hosted matches.

Here is a realistic cost picture from beginner through CD title:

| Expense | Typical Range |
|---|---|
| Beginner or CGC class (6-8 weeks) | $100 to $200 |
| Novice class (6-8 weeks) | $125 to $250 |
| Practice matches before trialing (3-5 recommended) | $10 to $20 each |
| Trial entries (3 qualifying scores needed; plan for 6-10 attempts) | $150 to $350 total |
| Annual club membership | $30 to $75 |
| Total path to CD title | $400 to $875 |

Travel is the wildcard. In a dense tri-state region, most trials are within 30 to 90 minutes. In rural areas, a single trial weekend can add $150 to $400 in gas and lodging. That gap in trial access is one of the concrete reasons multi-state club networks matter for competitive handlers.

## How do you find and join a dog obedience club?

The AKC maintains a club-finder tool at akc.org that lets you search by sport (obedience) and zip code. Most member clubs list their class schedules, membership requirements, and trial calendars there.

What to look for before you sign up:

**Attend a practice match first.** Most clubs run fun matches or training matches open to non-members. Watch how instructors give feedback. Fast, specific, correction-based communication is good coaching. Vague encouragement with no technical direction is a sign the instruction does not have depth.

**Check the club's trial history.** A club that hosts AKC licensed trials meets AKC facility and equipment standards. Training in those conditions means you will know the ring surface, jump heights, and distraction levels before your first real trial.

**Ask about class size and instructor experience.** Classes over eight dogs without a co-instructor dilute the individual feedback time. Ask whether any instructors hold or have worked dogs to Utility or beyond. Someone who has trained through UD teaches Novice differently than someone who has only competed at that level.

**Confirm your dog's eligibility.** AKC obedience trials are open to all dogs, including mixed breeds, through the AKC Canine Partners program. Mixed breeds and unregistered purebreds must enroll in Canine Partners before entering licensed events. There is no size, weight, or breed restriction across obedience classes. Spayed and neutered dogs compete without restriction.

The Certification Council for Professional Dog Trainers publishes a trainer directory at [ccpdt.org](https://www.ccpdt.org/) where you can search for certified instructors by specialty, including competition obedience. CCPDT candidates must document at least 300 hours of professional experience and pass a knowledge-based exam before earning the credential. Not every club instructor holds this certification, but finding one who does raises the floor on instruction quality.

## How long does it take a dog to earn a CD title?

Most handlers working with a novice dog take 12 to 24 months from starting structured training to their first qualifying score. Handlers with competition experience who get a trained-from-scratch dog can move faster, sometimes inside six months. Handlers who train inconsistently or rarely trial take three years or more.

A realistic phase-by-phase schedule for targeting a first CD within 18 months:

1. Months 1 to 3: foundation work at home and in a basic class. Loose-leash walking, solid sit and down, name recognition at distance.
2. Months 4 to 6: beginner club class or CGC prep. Introduce distraction work, practice stays in new locations.
3. Months 7 to 9: Novice class at the club. Work the six Novice exercises with trial-level criteria. Enter at least two practice matches.
4. Months 10 to 12: trial conditioning. Proof the long sit and long down in environments the dog finds harder. Clock every exercise to build consistency.
5. Months 13 to 18: enter licensed trials. Target the first qualifying score. Identify the weak exercise per run and fix it before the next trial.

The long sit and long down break more first-time Novice runs than any other exercise. Handlers practice them at home, where the dog is comfortable, and skip proofing them in strange buildings with unfamiliar dogs one mat away. Any dog that can hold a long down only at home is not ready to trial on it.

## What does the tri-state club structure add that a local class cannot?

Trial access. To earn a CD, you need three qualifying scores under at least two different judges. In a tri-state region such as the NY/NJ/CT corridor, the PA/NJ/DE overlap, or the OH/WV/KY metro areas, you can typically find a licensed trial within a 90-minute drive on most weekends between September and June. That means you can accumulate qualifying scores and feedback in months rather than years.

Tri-state networks also concentrate experienced handlers. A regional club circuit is likely to include handlers who have earned UDX or OTCH (Obedience Trial Championship) titles, which is the sport's highest competitive designation. The OTCH is awarded on a points system based on placements in Open B and Utility B; it requires consistent wins against other titled dogs, and fewer than 2,000 dogs have earned one since the title was established. Handlers at that level teach Novice with entirely different expectations than someone who has only trained to CD.

The other practical advantage is regional mentorship. In a healthy tri-state club circuit, handlers who have competed for years show up at matches to watch and give feedback. That informal coaching layer is what most online or big-box training programs cannot replicate.

## FAQ

**Can a mixed-breed dog compete in AKC obedience?**

Yes. Mixed breeds compete through the AKC Canine Partners program. Enrollment requires a one-time fee and a photo submission. Enrolled dogs receive a registration number and can enter all AKC obedience events, including licensed trials where qualifying scores count toward titles. There is no disadvantage in scoring or judging criteria compared to registered purebreds.

**What is the difference between a practice match and a licensed trial?**

A practice match uses AKC obedience rules but scores do not count toward titles. The point is feedback and ring experience under simulated trial conditions. A licensed trial is an official AKC event where qualifying scores go on record. Do at least two or three matches before entering a licensed trial. The match is where you want your dog to break a stay or blow a recall. The trial is where you want to find out it has stopped doing that.

**What does NQ mean and how common is it?**

NQ stands for Non-Qualifying. A dog NQs when it scores below 170, earns a zero on any exercise, or commits a disqualifying act (such as biting or leaving the ring). First-time trial runs with green dogs NQ at a high rate. That is not a problem; it is data. Every NQ tells you which exercise to proof harder before the next entry.

**Is there a pass/fail for individual exercises or just the overall score?**

Both. The run requires 170 or higher total AND no zero on any individual exercise. A zero on the recall NQs the run even if the dog scored 169 on everything else. Judges score each exercise on a per-exercise points-available basis and deduct for specific errors, so every run produces a point-by-point breakdown of where the dog cost itself points. That scoresheet is the training plan for the next session.

**How do I know if an obedience club near me is AKC-affiliated?**

Search the AKC club finder at akc.org using your zip code and filter for obedience as the sport. Clubs that appear there have applied for and received AKC member club or licensed-club status. Non-affiliated clubs can still offer excellent instruction, but if your goal is AKC titles, training with a club that runs or regularly attends licensed trials shortens the gap between where you train and where you compete.

## Bottom line

Tri state dog obedience clubs give handlers who want to compete the two things a standard pet class cannot: instruction built around AKC trial criteria and a regional schedule dense enough to accumulate qualifying scores in a reasonable time frame. The rules are fixed everywhere (200 points, 170 to qualify, no zeros), but the quality of instruction and the frequency of trial access vary substantially by region. Find a club through the AKC club finder, attend a practice match before enrolling, and confirm your instructor has trained dogs to Utility level or beyond. That combination gives your dog a real shot at its first title within 18 months of starting structured work.
