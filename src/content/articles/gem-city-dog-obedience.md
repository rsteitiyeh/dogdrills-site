Dog obedience classes teach five core behaviors over 6-8 weeks: sit, down, stay, come when called, and loose-leash walking. A quality curriculum at a Gem City dog obedience club, a community rec center, or a private trainer in any other city covers the same fundamentals. What varies is trainer credentials, class size, and how consistently you practice between sessions.

Most people underestimate that last part. The class is the instruction. You are the training. A dog who attends every session but never practices at home makes a fraction of the progress of a dog whose owner puts in five minutes twice a day. Set that expectation before your first night.

## What do dog obedience classes actually teach?

The foundation is five behaviors that every quality program covers: sit, down, stay, come when called, and walking on a loose leash. These are not arbitrary choices. They are the behaviors that keep a dog safe, manageable in public, and out of shelters.

Quality programs layer in additional skills beyond the core five:

- Name recognition at distance (your dog turns and looks at you when you say its name across a parking lot)
- Greeting people without jumping
- "Leave it" for food on the ground, other dogs, or anything the dog should not touch
- Basic impulse control, waiting at doorways and sitting before meals

Group classes provide something private sessions cannot replicate: your dog learns to pay attention to you while other dogs are five feet away. That is the real test. The park, the vet waiting room, the sidewalk on a Tuesday morning all involve exactly that situation. A dog who performs reliably in your living room but falls apart the moment another dog appears has a trained behavior in one context, not a trained behavior. Group classes train specifically for the environment where the skill has to work.

Puppy classes (typically for dogs 8-16 weeks old) add a socialization dimension that is just as important as the commands. The American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior recommends puppies begin socialization classes as early as 7-8 weeks of age, one week after their first vaccination ([AVSAB position statement on puppy socialization](https://avsab.org/resources/position-statements/)). The developmental window before 16 weeks is when positive social experiences have the most lasting effect. Missing it doesn't end a dog's social life, but recovering from poor early socialization takes far more repetitions than getting it right the first time.

## How long does it take to train a dog in obedience?

A 6-8 week basic course builds the foundation. Add another 6-8 weeks at the intermediate level and most dogs with daily at-home practice perform reliably in distracting public environments. That is the realistic timeline for an owner who trains consistently between classes.

The AKC Canine Good Citizen (CGC) program provides the clearest national benchmark for obedience skills. Its 10-item evaluation tests a dog on sitting politely for petting by a stranger, walking through a crowd without pulling, reacting calmly to another dog at close range, and holding a three-minute down-stay with the handler completely out of sight ([AKC Canine Good Citizen Program](https://www.akc.org/products-services/training-programs/canine-good-citizen/)). Most trainers recommend completing a basic-plus-intermediate sequence before testing. The CGC costs roughly $10-$20 per evaluation attempt and some housing complexes and therapy-dog organizations require it as documented proof of training.

Dogs who earn the CGC represent a concrete, verifiable training milestone rather than the vague claim of being "well-trained." If you want a goal to train toward, this is the one worth targeting.

Waiting until 6 months to start training is the most common mistake first-time owners make. You can train a dog after the early developmental window closes, but expect more repetitions to cement new behaviors.

## What skills does each training level cover?

The typical progression from puppy class through advanced work follows a clear skill ladder. Here is what you should see at each level:

| Level | Typical Age or Entry | Core Skills Taught | Class Duration |
|-------|---------------------|-------------------|----------------|
| Puppy class | 8-16 weeks | Sit, down, name response, socialization with people and dogs | 4-6 weeks |
| Basic obedience | Any age, no prior training | Sit, down, stay, recall, loose-leash walking | 6-8 weeks |
| Intermediate | Completed basic course | Heel, sit-stay at distance, down-stay out of sight, "leave it" around distractions | 6-8 weeks |
| Advanced / CGC prep | Completed intermediate | All 10 CGC behaviors, crowd work, polite greetings, 3-minute out-of-sight stay | 6-8 weeks |
| Canine Good Citizen test | Advanced-level training | 10-item pass/fail evaluation by AKC-approved evaluator | One test session |

Most dogs and owners benefit from at least two courses (basic plus intermediate) before testing for the CGC. Skipping straight to the evaluation after a single basic course typically results in a dog who can do the skills at home but falls apart in a test environment with a stranger handling them.

## How do you pick a qualified dog obedience trainer?

Credentials matter more than Yelp stars. The Certification Council for Professional Dog Trainers (CCPDT) is the most rigorous independent certification in North America. CCPDT candidates must document at least 300 hours of professional experience and pass a knowledge-based examination before earning the credential. Use the CCPDT's find-a-trainer directory at [ccpdt.org](https://www.ccpdt.org/) to locate certified trainers by location and specialty.

Before committing to any program, ask these questions directly:

What methods do you use? Trainers who lead with positive reinforcement techniques have the most research support behind their approach. Trainers who default to prong collars or electronic collars as a first step, without discussion of alternatives, should prompt more follow-up questions, not fewer.

What is your class size? More than 8 dogs per instructor limits the individual feedback any dog and handler pair receives. Classes over 10 dogs need a co-trainer to maintain quality.

Can I contact two recent clients? Any trainer who hesitates at a reference request is worth questioning. Trainers who deliver consistent results welcome the conversation.

What does success look like at the end of this program? This question separates trainers who sell outcomes from trainers who teach skills. A good trainer describes specific behaviors your dog will perform reliably and a practice plan to get there. Vague promises of a "fully trained dog" are a red flag.

## What results can you realistically expect?

After a basic obedience course with daily practice, your dog should reliably sit and down on cue, walk without pulling on a six-foot leash in familiar environments, and come when called in low-distraction settings. That is the honest post-course benchmark, and it is worth saying plainly because some expectations get set too high by optimistic marketing.

Off-leash recall in a dog park or on a trail with squirrels is an advanced skill. Most dogs need six or more months of consistent work beyond the basics before that recall is reliable. Set that expectation from the start so a six-week course doesn't feel like a disappointment when you still can't call your dog off a squirrel.

Reactivity (lunging and barking at other dogs or strangers on walks) is a separate behavior problem from basic obedience. A standard group class is not the right tool for it. A reactive dog who attends a general group class typically becomes more stressed, not less, and disrupts the learning environment for other dogs in the class. If your dog reacts hard to other dogs or people, look specifically for a reactive dog class or private sessions with a trainer who has documented experience in behavior modification.

## Does age or breed affect training speed?

Adult dogs often learn faster than puppies because they hold attention longer and are less distracted by everything in the room. A three-year-old rescue with no prior training is not a lost cause. Most trainers who specialize in rehomed dogs will confirm that adult dogs with no formal training frequently progress faster than puppies in an equivalent program, simply because the focus is there.

Breed affects motivation, not capacity. Border collies and Belgian Malinois work for the activity itself. Beagles work for food. Basset hounds work on their own schedule. A trainer who understands what your specific dog finds genuinely rewarding, not what it "should" want, gets faster results. If your dog is wild for a tennis ball, the tennis ball is the training tool. If it's cooked chicken, use the chicken. The reinforcer is the lever. Choosing the right one cuts training time measurably compared to using arbitrary treats a dog is indifferent to.

The variables that actually predict training success are consistency of daily practice, quality of the reinforcement, and starting before bad habits become the dog's default response.

## FAQ

**How many times a week should I train my dog at home?**
Five to ten minutes twice a day beats one 30-minute session on the weekend. Short, frequent sessions match a dog's attention window and build fluency faster. Missing one day costs almost nothing. Missing two weeks shows up clearly at the next class as regression in behaviors the dog had nailed.

**Can an adult dog still learn basic obedience?**
Yes, and often faster than a puppy. Adult dogs hold attention longer, respond reliably to higher-value reinforcement without getting overstimulated, and frequently generalize learned behaviors across new environments more quickly than young dogs do. The idea that dogs older than 6 months can't be trained is wrong in practice.

**What is the AKC Canine Good Citizen test and should my dog take it?**
The CGC is a 10-item skills evaluation administered by AKC-approved evaluators at training clubs and facilities across the country. It certifies that a dog meets a baseline standard of manners in public settings. Testing costs roughly $10-$20 per attempt. Some housing complexes and therapy-dog organizations require it as documented proof of training. It is a concrete, measurable goal worth training toward, and the skills it requires are exactly the ones a solid basic-plus-intermediate program covers. Details at the [AKC CGC program page](https://www.akc.org/products-services/training-programs/canine-good-citizen/).

**What if my dog can't keep up with the group class?**
A good trainer flags the mismatch by week two, not week six. If the class format isn't working because your dog is overwhelmed, reactive, or the pace is off for where the dog is in its training, switching to private sessions is the correct move, not a sign of failure. Ask for honest feedback after the second session and ask specifically whether your dog is progressing at the rate the trainer expects.

**How much does dog obedience training cost overall?**
Budget $200-$500 for a full basic-plus-intermediate sequence in group classes, depending on your region and program length. Private sessions run $75-$150 per hour. Board-and-train programs for more serious behavioral issues typically cost $1,500-$3,000 for a two-to-three week stay. Get quotes from at least two CCPDT-certified trainers before committing to any program.

## Bottom line

Before signing up at a Gem City dog obedience club or any program elsewhere, ask the same three questions: What methods do you use? What are your credentials? What does my dog need to perform reliably at the end of this course? Verify the trainer's certification at ccpdt.org, confirm they lead with positive reinforcement, and commit to the daily practice that makes any program actually work. The class gives you the tools. What you do with them in the 23 hours between sessions is the training.
