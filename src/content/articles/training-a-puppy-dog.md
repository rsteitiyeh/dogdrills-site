Training a puppy dog starts the day you bring them home, ideally at 8 weeks old. Short sessions of 3 to 5 minutes using food rewards teach sit, name recognition, and come faster than any other method. Most puppies can hold their first five commands reliably by 12 weeks when owners train consistently every single day.

Why does timing matter so much? A puppy's brain sits in a critical learning window from roughly 3 to 14 weeks. Skills built during that window wire into long-term memory more efficiently than skills learned at 5 or 6 months. That window starts closing around week 14, which is why waiting until your puppy "grows up a little" is one of the costliest mistakes new owners make.

## When should you start training a puppy dog?

Start at 8 weeks. That is the earliest most puppies come home, and it is the right time to begin. A puppy can learn name recognition and simple cues like sit within the first few days of arrival.

Some owners wait because they think the puppy needs to "settle in" first. Give the puppy 48 hours to decompress and then start. Not two months. The American Kennel Club puts 8 to 10 weeks as the prime window for foundational commands and notes that puppies who miss early training often develop problem behaviors that take significantly longer to fix ([AKC Puppy Training Timeline](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/training/puppy-training-timeline/)).

Puppies can also attend socialization classes starting at 7 to 8 weeks, roughly one week after their first set of vaccinations. The American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior updated its position on this years ago: the behavioral cost of keeping puppies isolated during their sensitive period outweighs the disease risk in well-run, sanitized puppy class settings. Early socialization is not optional; it is foundational.

## What commands should you teach a puppy dog first?

Teach sit first, then name recall, then down. Those three form the base that everything else builds on.

Here is why that order works. "Sit" is the easiest behavior for a puppy to offer accidentally while you lure with a treat, which means you can mark and reward it within the first session. Once sit is solid, you pair "name" with eye contact, which conditions the puppy to look at you before doing anything else. "Down" comes after because it requires the puppy to lower its whole body, a position that can feel vulnerable to a young dog who has not yet built trust with the training process.

After those three are reliable, add "stay" in tiny increments (1 second, then 3, then 5) and "come" with high-value rewards every single time. Never call a puppy to you for anything unpleasant in the first months. Come means good things happen, period.

Skip tricks like "shake" or "spin" until the five core commands are solid. Tricks feel like progress but they do not build the impulse control you need when real-world distractions show up at 6 months.

## How long should puppy training sessions run?

Keep sessions to 3 to 5 minutes each, three to five times a day. Puppies have short attention spans and fatigue fast mentally. A 4-minute session that ends on a clear success is worth more than a 20-minute session that ends with the puppy wandering off and ignoring you.

Timing within sessions matters too. End on a win. If you are working on "down" and the puppy nails it on the fourth repetition, reward big and stop. Pushing for ten more reps means the last three will be sloppy, and that sloppiness is what the puppy's memory consolidates overnight.

Build duration gradually. A 10-minute session is fine at 16 weeks with a puppy that has been training consistently. A 15-minute session at 5 months is reasonable. But in the first four to six weeks, frequency beats duration every time. Three 4-minute sessions beat one 12-minute session, even though the total minutes are the same.

## How do you handle biting during puppy training?

Stop the session, turn your back, and ignore the puppy for 10 to 15 seconds the moment it bites during training. Resume only when the puppy is calm. Do not yell, do not hold the muzzle shut, do not flick the nose.

Puppy biting during training usually signals one of three things: the puppy is overstimulated, the puppy is frustrated because the reward rate is too low, or the puppy is simply tired.

Overstimulated puppies bite at hands moving with treats. Slow your movements, use a treat pouch so hands are not the food dispenser, and lower the excitement level of your rewards temporarily.

Frustrated biters need faster reward rates. If the puppy has been attempting a new behavior for more than 30 seconds without getting a reward, lower the criteria and hand them a win. Frustration biting is the puppy telling you the gap between tries and rewards is too wide.

Tired puppies need a nap. At 8 to 12 weeks, a puppy that has been awake for more than 45 minutes is running on empty. Training a tired puppy teaches nothing and builds a negative association with the training environment.

## What does a good puppy training schedule look like by age?

Use this as your starting roadmap. Adjust up if your puppy is ahead; adjust down if they need more time on a skill before moving forward.

| Age | Main Focus | Session Length | Skills to Add |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8 to 10 weeks | Name, sit, body handling | 3 min, 4 to 5x daily | Name recognition, sit, touch tolerance |
| 10 to 12 weeks | Recall, leash introduction | 3 to 5 min, 4x daily | Come, leash on and off, collar grabs |
| 12 to 16 weeks | Down, stay (1 to 3 seconds) | 5 min, 3 to 4x daily | Down, short stay, impulse control games |
| 4 to 6 months | Stay (up to 30 seconds), polite greetings | 5 to 10 min, 3x daily | Heel starts, leave it, sit at the door |
| 6 to 12 months | Distraction and distance proofing | 10 to 15 min, 2x daily | Off-leash recall, real-world obedience |

This schedule is a floor, not a ceiling. If your puppy masters sit in one session, move on the same day. Ages here reflect typical developmental stages based on documented puppy learning curves, not arbitrary checkpoints.

## Does crate training count as training a puppy dog?

Yes, and it is one of the highest-leverage skills in the first weeks. The crate serves two training functions: it gives the puppy a safe space to decompress between sessions, and it accelerates house training because puppies avoid soiling where they sleep.

The common mistake is expecting too much too soon. A 9-week-old puppy cannot hold its bladder for more than 2 to 3 hours. An 8-week-old is closer to 1 to 2 hours. The ASPCA recommends a rough guideline of one hour per month of age plus one as a starting point for how long a puppy can stay crated during the day ([ASPCA Dog Behavior Resources](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/dog-care/dog-behavior-problems)).

Start crate conditioning before you need to use the crate. Feed meals inside it with the door open. Toss treats in randomly throughout the day. Build the positive association before you ever close that door. Puppies crated before they have built a good feeling about the space turn crate training into a weeks-long battle of crying and scratching.

## What mistakes slow down training a puppy dog?

The three biggest: inconsistent rules across family members, rewarding the wrong moment, and training only in one location.

Inconsistent rules mean the puppy gets on the couch on weekdays but not weekends. The puppy learns the outcome is random and keeps trying. Dogs get faster at behaviors when outcomes are predictable. Pick the rules and hold every person in the house to them.

Rewarding the wrong moment is a timing error that beginners make constantly. You say "sit," the puppy sits, then stands back up, and you say "yes" while the puppy is already standing. You just rewarded standing. In the first weeks, timing precision builds vocabulary. Sloppy timing builds confusion. Mark the exact moment the behavior happens: the instant the rear hits the floor, not two seconds later.

Training only in one room makes the puppy an expert at one context. A puppy that sits reliably in the kitchen may have no idea what "sit" means in the backyard the first time you ask there. Generalize deliberately: kitchen, then hallway, then front step, then yard, then driveway, then park. Each new location causes a short regression. Work through it fast with a higher reward rate and fewer distractions.

## Frequently asked questions about training a puppy dog

**Can you start training a puppy dog at 8 weeks?**
Yes, 8 weeks is the ideal starting age. A puppy this age can learn name recognition and a reliable sit within days of arriving home. Keep sessions to 3 minutes each and use high-value food rewards. Waiting until 12 or 16 weeks means missing the easiest window for foundational learning.

**How many times a day should you train a puppy?**
Three to five short sessions per day works best for puppies under 4 months. Keep each session to 3 to 5 minutes. Frequency matters more than session length at this stage. As focus improves, reduce frequency and increase session length gradually rather than jumping straight to longer workouts.

**What is the fastest way to train a puppy dog?**
Use small, high-value food rewards (chicken bits, cheese, or purpose-made training treats), keep sessions under 5 minutes, end every session on a correct behavior, and train in multiple locations from week one. Consistency from all household members cuts confusion and speeds progress more than any individual training technique.

**How do you train a puppy dog not to bite?**
Remove all attention for 10 to 15 seconds the instant the puppy bites during play or training. Return to interaction only when the puppy is calm. Over several weeks of consistent repetition, this teaches bite inhibition. Avoid physical corrections: they raise arousal and worsen biting in most puppies rather than reducing it.

**When should I enroll my puppy in a training class?**
Most puppy classes accept dogs starting at 7 to 8 weeks old, one week after their first vaccination. Veterinary behavior organizations recommend early enrollment specifically because the socialization benefits during weeks 8 to 14 are substantial, and a well-run class environment managed by a qualified trainer carries low disease risk compared to the behavioral cost of isolation.

Bottom line: training a puppy dog is a high-repetition, short-session game played in the first few months of life. Get the schedule right, mark the correct moment, use rewards worth working for, and switch locations often. The puppies that turn into rock-solid adult dogs are almost always the ones whose owners started early and trained often, not the ones who waited for the perfect time.
