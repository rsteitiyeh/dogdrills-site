The Sandia Dog Obedience Club (SDOC) is an AKC member club in Albuquerque, New Mexico that runs group obedience classes, Rally courses, conformation training, and licensed AKC trials. Any owner, any breed, any mix can attend a class or enter a trial without being a member first.

You likely searched for this club by name, which means you want real information: what classes they run, how to get your dog into their trials, what the costs look like, and whether club training actually beats working with a private trainer. This article covers all of that. It also explains how AKC member clubs work in general, so you can size up SDOC or any similar club in your area.

## What does a dog obedience club actually offer?

An AKC member club like SDOC offers four things: structured group classes from puppy through advanced levels, licensed AKC trials where dogs earn official titles, specialty programs like Rally and conformation, and a community of handlers who train and compete year-round. That last item gets undersold. Training around serious competitors raises your standards faster than working alone.

At SDOC, classes run on a session schedule, typically 6 to 8 weeks per session. New handlers can jump into the current curriculum or wait for the next session to open. The facility has an indoor training area and ring space set up to mirror what their own licensed trials use. That specificity matters because dogs trained in the same environment where they will compete carry fewer unknowns into the ring.

SDOC also runs CGC (Canine Good Citizen) evaluations. The CGC is a 10-skill pass/fail test administered by an AKC Evaluator and is the most practical starting point for owners who are not ready for obedience competition but want a real, measurable goal to work toward. SDOC can connect you with an evaluation date.

## How do AKC obedience titles work at a local club?

AKC obedience titles require three qualifying scores at three licensed trials under at least two different judges. A qualifying score is 170 or above out of 200 possible points, with no single exercise scoring below 50 percent of its maximum value. Local clubs like SDOC hold the trials where those qualifications happen.

SDOC runs its own AKC-sanctioned obedience and Rally trials, typically twice a year in spring and fall. That schedule lets a member chase three qualifying legs without leaving Albuquerque. Confirm upcoming trial dates, download the premium list, and submit entries through the [AKC Obedience events page](https://www.akc.org/sports/obedience/). Close dates typically land 4 to 6 weeks before the trial, so check early.

The full title ladder in AKC Obedience:

- CD (Companion Dog): Novice level, on and off-leash heeling, stand for exam, recall, group exercises
- CDX (Companion Dog Excellent): Open level, adds retrieve on flat and over jump, broad jump, drop on recall
- UD (Utility Dog): scent discrimination, hand signals, directed jumping, directed retrieve
- UDX (Utility Dog Excellent): qualifies in both Open B and Utility B at the same trial ten times
- OTCH (Obedience Trial Champion): point-based system requiring wins over other qualifying dogs; fewer than 200 dogs nationally earn it in a given year, according to AKC records

Rally runs a parallel track with its own title series (RN, RA, RE, RAE, RACH) and is generally faster to earn at the entry level because handlers can talk to the dog throughout the course.

## What class levels does the Sandia Dog Obedience Club offer?

SDOC runs class levels that match the AKC competitive structure, from foundational puppy work through Utility. The table below shows what each level builds and where it leads:

| Class Level | Core Skills | AKC Title Path | Best For |
|---|---|---|---|
| Puppy (8-16 wks) | Focus, sit, down, leash intro, bite inhibition | Foundation only | New puppy owners |
| Beginner Novice | Sit, down, stay, recall, loose-leash walk | BN title | Adult dogs new to training |
| Novice | On/off-leash heel, figure-8, stand for exam, long sit/down, recall | CD title | Handlers ready to trial |
| Open | Drop on recall, retrieve on flat and over jump, broad jump | CDX title | CD holders advancing |
| Utility | Scent discrimination, directed retrieve, hand signals, directed jumping | UD, UDX, OTCH | Advanced competitive handlers |
| Rally | 10-20 numbered stations per course, handler may speak to dog | RN, RA, RE, RAE | All levels, fast progression |

Pick your entry point honestly. If your dog holds a sit-stay with mild distractions for 30 seconds and walks on a loose leash, Novice is the right start. If that falls apart during the first session, drop to Beginner Novice. Ego about class placement is the single most common reason handlers spend 18 months on a path that should take six.

## How much does it cost to join SDOC and train with the club?

Annual SDOC membership runs roughly $25 to $60 for individual owners. Dues adjust over time, so verify the current rate at sandiadogs.org before joining. Class fees typically land at $80 to $160 per 6 to 8 week session. AKC trial entry fees run $25 to $35 per class per dog per day.

Compare those numbers to private one-on-one training in Albuquerque, which runs $75 to $150 per session. A club class works out to $10 to $20 per meeting. The tradeoff is direct: a private trainer gives you undivided attention for one hour, a club gives you volume, distraction training, and ring-replica repetitions at a fraction of the hourly cost.

Trial entry fees add up if you're competing in multiple classes. Entering Novice A on Saturday and Novice A on Sunday at the same trial costs two entry fees. SDOC trials cap entries to keep wait times reasonable, which means popular classes fill fast. Entering within the first few days of the opening date is not overreaction; it's how you secure a spot.

Members get priority entry at SDOC's own trials before slots open to the public. In a sport where a Friday deadline closes out by Tuesday, that priority is worth more than the annual dues.

## How do you join the Sandia Dog Obedience Club?

Joining SDOC starts with attending a class as a guest or pulling the current membership application from sandiadogs.org. Most AKC member clubs run a brief process: complete the application, pay annual dues, and attend a set number of club events before receiving full voting membership.

Full membership gives you reduced class rates, priority trial entry at SDOC events, voting rights at club meetings, and access to members-only match days. Practice matches are informal run-throughs under trial conditions, and they are one of the most valuable resources a club offers. Getting your dog into a ring environment before the licensed trial removes a huge variable.

Non-members can still enter SDOC-hosted AKC trials as listed entries, but those spots fill after member entries close. Check the AKC Event Search (akc.org/events) for upcoming SDOC trial dates, entry deadlines, and class offerings.

## Is competitive obedience club training right for every dog?

Club training is not the right fit for every dog right now. Dogs with active leash reactivity, fear-based responses to crowds, or physical conditions affecting gait need individual behavior work before a group class will help. A good club tells you this directly at intake rather than take your money for a session that sets the dog back.

That said, the range of dogs that succeed in club training is wider than most owners assume. The AKC's [Canine Good Citizen program](https://www.akc.org/products-services/training-programs/canine-good-citizen/) has awarded over 1 million titles since the program launched in 1989. The CGC is achievable for nearly any adult dog with 8 to 12 weeks of consistent work, and it opens the door to Rally and Novice obedience from there.

The honest block is rarely the dog. A dog that looks sloppy in the ring usually has a handler who cues late, drifts off the center line, or blocks the dog's path with their body. Clubs fix that because instructors and fellow members will tell you what they see. That kind of feedback rarely happens in private lessons, where clients push back.

Mixed-breed dogs compete in all SDOC performance events through the AKC Canine Partners program. They are not eligible for conformation, but every obedience, Rally, and agility title is available. Register a mixed-breed dog at akc.org/canine-partners before submitting a trial entry.

## How does training at SDOC compare to training at home or with a private trainer?

Club training adds three things a backyard session cannot replicate: a distraction-heavy environment, informed observers, and ring-like conditions. Dogs trained exclusively at home often learn to perform only at home. The smell of a training building, other dogs working nearby, strangers watching ringside, and the specific floor surface all activate competing stimuli the dog has never had to work through.

SDOC's training ring is set up to match its own trial ring. A dog that holds a 30-second group sit in your living room may break at 10 seconds in a building with 40 dogs and a judge walking the line. Clubs train that variable out. Repetition in the actual environment is the whole point.

Use both formats when you can. Club classes for ring prep, trial repetition, and distraction work. Private trainers for a specific mechanical problem (a wide heel, a slow retrieve, a sticky front position) that needs concentrated drilling on one skill. They solve different problems and work better together than either does alone.

## FAQ

**How do I find the SDOC trial schedule?**
Go to the AKC Event Search at akc.org/events, enter "Sandia" or "Albuquerque" in the location field, and filter by Obedience or Rally. The premium list and entry form download directly from the event listing. Trial entries typically close 4 to 6 weeks before the event date.

**Can my mixed-breed dog train and compete with SDOC?**
Yes. Mixed-breed dogs are welcome in all SDOC classes and can compete in obedience, Rally, agility, and tracking under the AKC Canine Partners program. Conformation is the only exception. Enroll your mixed-breed dog at akc.org/canine-partners before entering any licensed trial.

**How many lessons before a dog is ready to enter a trial?**
Plan on at least one complete class session at the level you intend to enter, plus at least one practice match. A practice match is an informal run-through under trial conditions. Do not enter a licensed trial until your dog holds all group exercises reliably around other dogs. Entering too early can set back ring confidence by months.

**What exactly is a qualifying score in AKC obedience?**
A qualifying score is 170 or above out of a possible 200 points. Each individual exercise must also score at least 50 percent of its maximum point value. A zero on any exercise is a non-qualifying run regardless of the total. Judges deduct for position errors, timing, and precision faults. Consistent mechanical work produces consistent scores.

**What if we are not aiming for competition titles?**
Many SDOC members train purely to have a well-behaved, mentally engaged dog and never enter a trial. The instruction is the same either way. Training through Utility-level exercises is one of the most effective ways to keep a working-breed dog from becoming a problem dog. The title is the receipt; the training is what you actually bought.
