For the first week of overnight crate training, place the crate in your bedroom, close enough that your puppy can hear and smell you, then move it toward its permanent location in small increments over the following two to three weeks once your puppy is sleeping through settling periods without prolonged distress. A puppy who cries all night usually needs either a location change, a shorter distance to you, or a genuine unmet need addressed, not more time ignored.

This is a different problem than nightly routine mechanics. If you already know your feeding cutoffs and potty-break timing, what typically breaks first-week success is crate placement and inconsistent night-to-night handling. Get the location and the week-one plan right and most puppies stabilize by night four or five.

## Where should the crate go for the first week?

Put the crate in your bedroom, ideally within a few feet of your bed, for the entire first week regardless of where you eventually want it long-term. Proximity to you reduces the separation distress that drives most first-week crying, since puppies are used to sleeping in a litter pile and total isolation is a big ask on night one.

1. Set the crate up before the puppy arrives if possible, so it's a known object, not a surprise on night one.
2. Position it so your puppy can see or smell you from inside, but you are not making eye contact or engaging once lights are out.
3. Avoid the kitchen, laundry room, or any far-off "puppy area" for week one, even if that's the eventual plan. Isolation on night one sets back the whole week.
4. Keep the same crate location for at least 5 to 7 consecutive nights before considering any move. Changing location night to night resets the puppy's sense of a stable den.

## How do I move the crate to its permanent spot without restarting the crying?

Move the crate a short distance, roughly one room's worth of progress every 3 to 4 nights, only after your puppy has had at least two consecutive calm nights at the current spot. Moving too early re-triggers the isolation distress you just solved.

1. Confirm two calm nights (minimal crying, no prolonged distress) at the current location before moving at all.
2. Move the crate just outside the bedroom door for the next stage if your end goal is a hallway or nearby room, rather than jumping straight to a far room.
3. Leave the bedroom door open at this stage so sound and scent still travel.
4. Repeat: two calm nights, then move again, room by room, until you reach the target location.
5. If crying restarts significantly after a move, move it back one stage for a few more nights before trying again. This isn't a failure, it's normal pacing.

## What does a realistic first-week night-by-night plan look like?

Expect the most crying on nights one and two, a rough middle stretch around night three as fatigue sets in for both of you, and a real improvement by night four or five as the routine becomes familiar. Use the table below as your baseline and adjust from there.

1. Night 1-2: Expect crying at bedtime and after each potty break. Keep responses calm, brief, and identical every time (no talking, no eye contact, straight to the potty spot and back).
2. Night 3: Often the hardest night. Fatigue is high and progress can feel invisible. Stick with the identical routine rather than changing anything out of frustration.
3. Night 4-5: Most puppies show a noticeable drop in crying duration and frequency as the pattern becomes predictable.
4. Night 6-7: If calm, this is your first opportunity to begin the gradual crate-location move described above.
5. Log each night briefly (time to settle, number of wake-ups, any accidents). Patterns across a week are more useful than judging any single bad night.

## My puppy cries the entire night, every night. What am I missing?

All-night, non-stopping crying that doesn't taper off after several nights usually points to one of a few specific gaps: crate too far from you, a genuine physical need not being met, or a crate that itself feels aversive due to size, past incidents, or being used as punishment.

1. Check crate size first: a crate too large or too small both increase discomfort and restlessness. It should fit a puppy standing, turning, and lying down, no more.
2. Check for punishment association: never put a puppy in the crate as a consequence for bad behavior. If that's happened, the crate needs to be re-associated with something good (meals, treats, calm time with the door open) before nighttime use will improve.
3. Rule out a genuine need being missed: reconfirm your potty-break timing is appropriate for age (see the AKC's general crate training guidance for baseline expectations, [source](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/health/how-to-crate-train-a-dog/)).
4. Check for littermate or previous-environment mismatch: puppies raised in group settings sometimes need a slower isolation taper, including a first few nights with the crate right next to your hand hanging down for contact.
5. If crying persists past two full weeks with no improvement trend at all, consider a consult with your vet or a certified trainer to rule out anxiety beyond typical adjustment (source: [AVSAB](https://www.avsab.org/)).

## First-Week Crate Location and Troubleshooting Plan

| Night | Crate location | Expected behavior | Action if off-track |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1-2 | Bedroom, near your bed | Crying at bedtime and after breaks | Keep responses calm and identical, do not move crate |
| 3 | Bedroom, same spot | Often the hardest night, fatigue peak | Do not change routine out of frustration |
| 4-5 | Bedroom, same spot | Crying should noticeably taper | If not tapering, check crate size and association |
| 6-7 | Bedroom, same spot | 2 calm nights unlocks first move | Begin moving toward target room, one stage at a time |
| Week 2+ | Gradually toward target room | Continued calm nights per stage | Move back one stage if crying spikes after a move |

## How does crate setup interact with a multi-pet household during the first week?

If you already have another dog or cat, that animal's presence and reaction to the new crate matters just as much as the crate's physical location. A resident pet who investigates aggressively or fixates on the crate can add stress on top of normal first-week adjustment.

1. Keep the crate in a spot where resident pets can approach calmly but not hover directly over it or paw at it during settling time.
2. Give the resident pet their own settled space nearby rather than closing them out entirely, since total separation sometimes increases curiosity and disruption at the crate.
3. Supervise the first few nights closely for any tension, and separate rooms entirely if a resident pet is overly aroused or anxious around the new puppy's crate.
4. Expect resident pets to adjust faster than the puppy typically does. Most settle into the new routine within the same week the puppy does.

## What first-week setup mistakes make the crying worse instead of better?

Certain well-intentioned choices in week one commonly backfire and extend the adjustment period rather than shortening it.

1. Moving the crate to a new spot every night to "find what works" resets progress each time instead of letting the puppy build familiarity with one location.
2. Leaving a bowl of water in the crate overnight, which increases the odds of an accident and disrupts sleep with more bathroom urgency.
3. Responding inconsistently, sometimes ignoring crying and sometimes rushing in, which confuses the puppy about what actually gets a response.
4. Using a crate that's too large for the current size of a young puppy, which removes the natural den effect that discourages soiling near the sleeping area.
5. Skipping the pre-bed calm-down period and going straight from high-energy play into the crate, which leaves a puppy overstimulated rather than ready to settle.

## FAQ

**Should the crate go in my bedroom or in the puppy's own room from day one?**
Start in your bedroom for at least the first week. Puppies separated from all human contact on night one tend to cry longer and harder, which can make the whole first week worse. You can move the crate gradually once your puppy is sleeping calmly.

**Is it normal for a puppy to cry every single night for the first week?**
Some crying on nights one through three is common and expected. What matters is a downward trend by night four or five. If crying stays at the same intensity with zero improvement after a full week, something specific (location, size, association) usually needs adjusting.

**Can I use a second crate to avoid carrying it between rooms during the move?**
Yes, a second identical crate at the target location works fine as long as bedding and a worn t-shirt with your scent move with the puppy, so the space still smells and feels familiar during the transition.

**How do littermates or shelter puppies handle overnight crating differently?**
Puppies raised with littermates or in group shelter settings often need a slower isolation taper since they are used to constant body contact at night. Extra proximity (crate right beside your bed, a hand resting near the crate door for the first few nights) tends to help more than it would for a singly-raised puppy.

**What if I have to keep the crate in a separate room from day one due to allergies or space?**
It's harder but workable. Compensate with a longer settling routine before bed, a piece of your worn clothing in the crate, and a baby monitor so you can hear and respond quickly to genuine distress even from another room.

## Bottom line

Overnight crate success in week one comes down to location, not just routine: start the crate in your bedroom, hold that spot for at least five to seven nights, and move it gradually in stages only after consecutive calm nights. A puppy who cries all night, every night, with no improvement usually has a specific fixable issue (size, distance, or a punishment association) rather than a temperament problem.
