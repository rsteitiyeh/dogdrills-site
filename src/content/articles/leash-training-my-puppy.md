Leash training a puppy starts indoors with short, positive collar and leash introductions before any actual walking happens, then progresses through following you, walking beside you in a distraction-free space, and finally walking outside with real-world distractions. Most puppies reach reasonably polite loose-leash walking within 3 to 6 weeks of daily short sessions, though full reliability around distractions can take longer.

Skipping the indoor groundwork is the single biggest reason puppies fight the leash outside. A puppy who has never worn a collar comfortably in the living room will absolutely fight it on a sidewalk full of new smells and sounds.

## How do I get my puppy used to wearing a collar or harness?

Put the collar or harness on for short periods during activities your puppy already enjoys, like meals or play, so it becomes associated with good things rather than restriction. Do this before you ever attach a leash.

1. Put the collar or harness on right before feeding time, then remove it after the meal. Repeat for 2 to 3 days.
2. Watch for the freeze-and-scratch reaction (many puppies do this initially). Distract with a toy or treat rather than fussing over it.
3. Extend wearing time gradually: an hour of supervised play, then most of the day, over the course of a week.
4. Check fit regularly: two fingers should fit snugly under the collar or harness strap. Puppies grow fast and need frequent adjustment checks.

## How do I introduce the leash without a fight?

Clip the leash on indoors and let your puppy drag it around under supervision first, with zero pulling from you, so the sensation of something attached becomes normal before you ever apply tension.

1. Clip the leash on during a calm indoor moment, not right before an exciting event like a walk.
2. Let your puppy walk around, drag the leash, and investigate it for 5 to 10 minutes while you stay nearby and quiet.
3. Pick up the leash and simply follow your puppy around the room for a minute or two, applying no tension at all.
4. Introduce very light, brief tension only if your puppy is comfortable: a gentle pull toward you, released the instant they take a step your way, paired with a treat.
5. Repeat this indoor stage for 2 to 3 days before attempting any actual directed walking.

## How do I teach loose-leash walking instead of pulling?

Reward your puppy for staying near your leg and stop moving forward entirely the instant the leash goes tight, so pulling is never rewarded with progress toward whatever they're excited about.

1. Start in a low-distraction space (your living room or backyard), not the street, for the first several sessions.
2. Hold treats at your side, at your puppy's nose height, and take a few steps. Reward every step or two that your puppy stays near your leg without pulling.
3. The instant the leash goes taut, stop walking completely. Do not yank back or drag your puppy. Simply become a stationary post.
4. Wait for slack to return to the leash (your puppy turns back toward you or the tension releases on its own), then mark and reward, and resume walking.
5. Repeat this stop-and-reward pattern consistently. It feels slow at first, sometimes only a few feet of progress per minute, but consistency here is what builds the habit (source: [AKC](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/training/)).
6. Once your puppy holds loose-leash walking reliably indoors and in the yard, move to a quiet street with minimal distractions before working up to busier areas.

## How do I handle a puppy who plants their feet and refuses to walk at all?

Refusal to move is usually fear or overstimulation, not defiance, especially in a new or busy environment. Do not drag a puppy who has planted their feet.

1. Crouch down to your puppy's level and encourage with a happy tone and a treat a few inches away, rather than pulling on the leash.
2. If they still won't move, back up to a spot they were comfortable in moments earlier and let them settle before trying again.
3. Shorten the outing. A 5-minute walk that ends calmly is more useful than a 20-minute walk that ends in a stressed, planted puppy.
4. Build exposure gradually: start in your own yard or driveway, then the sidewalk directly outside, before attempting a full neighborhood loop.
5. If planting persists across many sessions in familiar territory, consider whether the collar or harness itself is uncomfortable, or whether an underlying health issue could be limiting movement.

## What if my puppy bites or chews the leash during walks?

Leash biting is common in young puppies and is usually a mix of teething, overstimulation, and the leash being a novel, dangling object at exactly their eye level. Redirect rather than punish.

1. Carry a toy or have treats ready to redirect the instant your puppy grabs the leash, rather than pulling it out of their mouth (which often turns it into a tug game).
2. Stop moving when they grab it, similar to the pulling response, since continued forward motion can feel like a reward-adjacent game.
3. Consider a shorter leash or a leash with a bitter-taste deterrent spray if the behavior is frequent and disrupting walks.
4. Address general teething needs separately with appropriate chew toys, since a puppy who has an outlet for chewing at home often bites the leash less.

## Leash Training Stages and Timeline

| Stage | Goal | Typical duration | Ready to advance when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Collar/harness intro | Comfortable wearing it | 3-7 days | Puppy ignores it during normal activity |
| Leash drag (indoor) | Comfortable with attachment | 2-3 days | No freezing or frantic reaction to the leash |
| Following with slack | Comfortable with light tension | 3-5 days | Follows tension toward you without resistance |
| Loose-leash walking (low distraction) | Walking near your leg | 1-2 weeks | Holds position for full short walks indoors/yard |
| Loose-leash walking (outdoors) | Walking politely with real distractions | 2-4 weeks | Consistent slack leash on familiar routes |

## How do I handle distractions like other dogs, cars, or joggers during walks?

Distractions are the real test of loose-leash walking, and puppies need graduated exposure rather than being thrown straight into a busy environment. Trying to correct pulling toward a distraction with a full-grown dog's level of expectation on a puppy usually backfires.

1. Start distraction training at a distance where your puppy notices the trigger but can still respond to you, sometimes 20 to 30 feet away for a first exposure.
2. Reward attention toward you the instant your puppy notices the distraction but doesn't lunge or fixate, before they even have a chance to pull.
3. Gradually close the distance across multiple sessions rather than a single walk, moving closer only when your puppy stays responsive at the current distance.
4. If your puppy fixates hard and won't respond to treats or their name, you're too close. Increase distance rather than trying to power through with corrections.
5. Practice with predictable, lower-intensity distractions first (a parked car, a person standing still) before working up to unpredictable ones like joggers or excited dogs.

## Do different leash types or lengths make training easier or harder?

Yes, equipment choice affects how easy it is to teach and reinforce loose-leash walking, particularly in the early weeks when your puppy is still learning what leash tension means.

1. Use a standard 4 to 6 foot leash for training sessions. Retractable leashes teach the opposite lesson: that pulling extends their range, which directly undermines loose-leash training.
2. A front-clip harness can reduce pulling strength mechanically while you're still building the habit, redirecting a pulling puppy's momentum sideways rather than forward.
3. Avoid long lines for structured loose-leash sessions; save them for sniff walks and decompression time where pulling toward smells is actually fine.
4. Once loose-leash walking is reliable, you can introduce a longer leash for more relaxed walks without undoing the training, since the habit is already established.

## FAQ

**At what age can I start leash training a puppy?**
You can start collar and indoor leash introductions as early as 8 weeks old, well before full vaccination clears them for public walks. Save actual outdoor walking in public areas until your vet confirms it's safe based on your puppy's vaccination schedule.

**Why does my puppy walk fine at home but pull hard outside?**
New environments bring far more competing smells, sounds, and movement, and a behavior trained in a low-distraction space doesn't automatically transfer to a high-distraction one. Treat outdoor walking as a new skill built in stages: quiet street first, busier areas later.

**Should I use a harness or a collar for leash training a puppy?**
Both can work, but a well-fitted harness is generally easier on a puppy's developing neck and reduces the risk of injury from sudden pulls compared to a flat collar, particularly for small or brachycephalic breeds. A collar is fine for ID tags and light leash pressure once walking is reliable.

**How long should puppy leash training walks be at first?**
Keep initial leash walks to 5 to 10 minutes. Puppies tire quickly and short, successful sessions build better habits than long ones that end in frustration or exhaustion for either of you.

**Is it normal for puppy leash training to take over a month?**
Yes, especially for the outdoor, distraction-heavy phase. Indoor basics often click within one to two weeks, but confident, consistent loose-leash walking in varied real-world settings commonly takes 4 to 6 weeks or longer depending on the puppy's temperament and how often you practice.

## Bottom line

Leash training a puppy is a staged process: get them comfortable with the collar, then the leash, then following with slack, then walking beside you without pulling, starting indoors and moving outside only once each stage is solid. Stop moving the instant the leash goes tight rather than pulling back, and expect the full process to take 3 to 6 weeks of short, consistent daily sessions.
