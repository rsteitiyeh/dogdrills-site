These leash training tips for puppies all come back to one rule: stop moving the instant the leash goes tight. Begin at 8 weeks old, inside your home, with 5-minute sessions and high-value treats. Use a 4- to 6-foot leash, never a retractable. Most puppies achieve a reliable loose-leash walk in 4 to 6 weeks of daily practice.

Leash training is not about the leash. It is about teaching your puppy that staying near you pays better than charging ahead. Get that foundation right and every other leash skill follows quickly.

## When should you start leash training a puppy?

Start the day your puppy comes home, typically at 8 weeks old. The socialization window runs roughly from 3 to 14 weeks, and habits formed during this period stick harder than anything learned later. Waiting until 4 or 5 months means your puppy has already spent weeks practicing the wrong behavior, and undoing that takes three times as long.

The American Kennel Club recommends beginning basic training and leash introduction at 7 to 8 weeks, noting that puppies at this stage form new associations more quickly than at any other life stage ([AKC: Leash Training a Puppy](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/training/leash-training-a-puppy/)).

Keep early sessions short. At 8 weeks, 5 minutes is plenty. At 12 weeks, stretch to 10 minutes. A young puppy has the attention span of a toddler; ending the session before she checks out builds better habits than grinding through a long frustrated one.

## What equipment do you need for puppy leash training?

Two things: a collar or harness, and a leash. That is the full list.

**Collar vs. harness:** A flat buckle collar fits most puppies and is simple to use. Brachycephalic breeds (pugs, French bulldogs, English bulldogs) do better with a harness from the start to avoid airway pressure. Front-clip harnesses are particularly useful for puppies that already pull, because forward tension steers them sideways rather than forward. Avoid prong collars, choke chains, and shock collars on any puppy. They are not needed for teaching basic leash manners, and the startle response from a correction at that age can create fear-based behavior that takes months to work through.

**Leash:** Use a 4- to 6-foot nylon or leather leash. Put the retractable away. A retractable leash teaches your puppy that pulling produces more distance, the opposite message from the one you are trying to send.

| Item | Best choice | Skip |
|------|-------------|------|
| Collar | Flat buckle, 2-finger gap from neck | Prong, choke chain, or shock collar |
| Harness | Front-clip for pullers; back-clip for calm dogs | Cheap harnesses that restrict shoulder movement |
| Leash | 4-6 ft nylon or leather | Retractable leash |
| Treats | Soft, pea-sized: cooked chicken, string cheese | Hard biscuits (too slow to deliver mid-session) |

## How do you introduce a collar and leash to a puppy?

Rushing this step causes most of the resistance owners complain about later.

**Week 1: collar only.** Put the collar on right before your puppy's meal. Food distraction takes her focus off the strange sensation around her neck. Keep it on for 10 minutes, then remove. Repeat twice daily. By day 3 or 4, most puppies stop pawing at it.

**Week 2: leash drags free.** Clip the leash to the collar and let your puppy drag it around the house while you supervise. Do not hold the other end yet. This gets her used to the leash weight and the sound of the metal clip before you add any tension.

**Week 3: you hold the leash.** Pick up the leash and follow wherever your puppy goes, keeping the leash completely loose the entire time. No steering, no corrections. Just follow her. Reward with a treat every time she glances back at you or walks toward you on her own.

This three-week sequence is what most guides skip. The puppies that sit down in protest, spin around, or chew the leash during walks almost always skipped this stage.

## What is the step-by-step method for leash training a puppy?

Once your puppy is comfortable with the collar and leash, use this sequence to build actual loose-leash walking.

**Step 1: Teach your marker.** Before any leash work, teach your puppy that a specific word, say "yes," means a treat is coming. Say "yes," deliver a treat, repeat 20 times. One 3-minute session handles this.

**Step 2: Load the reward zone.** Hold a treat at your left hip. When your puppy moves beside your leg, say "yes" and feed the treat at your hip, not in front of you. You are teaching her exactly where the reward zone is.

**Step 3: One step at a time.** Take a single step forward. If she stays beside you, say "yes" and treat. If she moves ahead and the leash tightens, stop completely. No verbal correction, no leash tug. Just stop. Wait for the leash to go slack, then take one more step.

**Step 4: Build slowly.** Go from one step to two, then five, then ten. Reward every 2 to 3 steps at first. Once she is consistent, stretch rewards to every 5 to 6 steps. Do not skip ahead in this progression; that is the main cause of leash-training plateaus.

**Step 5: Move outdoors.** Start in your backyard or the quietest stretch of your street. The new environment will spike your puppy's excitement, so go back to rewarding every 2 to 3 steps. Expecting the same precision you got indoors, in a place with new smells and sounds, sets you both up to fail.

**Step 6: Introduce distractions systematically.** Parked cars first, then pedestrians, then other dogs at a distance. Each new distraction category means temporarily increasing your reward rate again until your puppy settles in.

## How long does leash training take for a puppy?

Most puppies show real improvement within 5 to 10 sessions. A reliable loose-leash walk in most outdoor settings typically takes 4 to 8 weeks of consistent daily practice.

| Week | Realistic milestone |
|------|---------------------|
| 1 | Wears collar without distress; drags leash indoors calmly |
| 2 | Follows you on a loose leash for 10 to 15 steps indoors |
| 3 | Stops when you stop; walks beside you for 20 to 30 steps |
| 4 | Loose-leash walking in the backyard, inconsistent but present |
| 6 | Reliable loose leash on quiet sidewalks |
| 8-10 | Walks well in moderate-distraction settings; still needs treats near other dogs |

Progress slower than this usually points to one of three things: sessions that run too long, treats that are not good enough, or practice environments that are too challenging too soon.

## Why does my puppy pull on the leash?

Because pulling has worked. Every time you followed your puppy when the leash was tight, you taught her that pulling gets results.

Pulling is not stubbornness or a dominance move. It is a behavior you reinforced by accident. Fix it by making pulling useless and walking calmly beside you extremely worthwhile.

**Method 1: Stop and wait.** The instant the leash tightens, you stop. You do not move until the leash goes slack again. The ASPCA recommends this approach as the core technique for teaching polite leash manners: when forward momentum stops every time the leash goes tight, the puppy learns that pulling does not work ([ASPCA: Leash Pulling](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/dog-care/common-dog-behavior-issues/leash-pulling)).

**Method 2: Turn and go the other way.** The instant the leash tightens, turn 180 degrees and walk in the opposite direction. Call your puppy cheerfully so she trots to catch up. Reward when she reaches your side. This method works faster for many puppies because the dog physically cannot drag you toward something you are walking away from.

Use your chosen method every single time the leash tightens. One exception per walk can set training back by days. Inconsistency is the only real reason these methods fail.

## What if my puppy bites or chews the leash?

Leash biting is nearly universal in puppies under 5 months old. They are teething, and a dangling leash looks exactly like a tug toy.

Do not yank the leash away. Yanking triggers prey drive and turns the whole thing into a game. Instead, keep a small chew toy in your free hand during walks. The instant you see your puppy eyeing the leash, produce the toy and guide it into her mouth before she can grab the leash. Feed it to her calmly; no waving or teasing. Most puppies stop targeting the leash within one to two weeks because it no longer produces any interesting response.

If the behavior is severe, switching to a leather leash (less appealing texture than nylon) or holding the leash higher to reduce how much loose material hangs near the puppy can help short term.

## FAQ

**At what age should you start leash training a puppy?**

Start at 8 weeks, the day your puppy comes home. The socialization window from 8 to 14 weeks is when new experiences become normal rather than alarming. A puppy introduced to a collar at 8 weeks treats it as part of daily life. One introduced for the first time at 5 months often treats it as a new threat that must be tested and resisted.

**Should I use a harness or collar for leash training a puppy?**

Either works for most breeds. Front-clip harnesses are better for puppies that already pull because tension redirects them sideways rather than forward. A flat buckle collar works fine for calm dogs or smaller breeds where pulling is not a strong habit yet. Skip back-clip harnesses for training because they give the puppy leverage to brace and pull harder.

**How long should puppy leash training sessions be?**

5 minutes at 8 to 10 weeks old. 10 minutes at 12 to 16 weeks. End the session before your puppy loses focus. Two short sessions per day produce better results than one long session that ends with a frustrated, overtired puppy making mistakes.

**Why does my puppy freeze and refuse to walk on leash?**

Freezing usually means the situation is too overwhelming. Do not drag the puppy forward. Crouch down, speak calmly, and toss a treat 10 to 12 inches in front of her. When she moves to get it, take one step together, then reward. If freezing happens on every single walk, spend another week on indoor leash work before heading outside again.

**Can I leash train a puppy without using treats?**

Treats are the fastest available teaching tool, but you can phase them out once the behavior is solid, typically around weeks 6 to 8. Real food, cooked chicken or small cubes of string cheese, works faster than most commercial training treats for most puppies. Once walking without pulling is reliable, shift to intermittent rewards and let the walk itself become the payoff.

---

**Bottom line:** The leash training tips for puppies that actually work are straightforward: start at 8 weeks, keep sessions short, and stop every time the leash goes tight. Skip methods involving corrections, retractable leashes, or waiting until the dog is older. Do the basics daily for 4 to 6 weeks and most puppies get there.
