The direct answer: withhold attention the instant your dog jumps (turn away, no eye contact, no touching or talking), then reward all four paws on the floor immediately when they happen, ideally by asking for a sit first. Consistency across every person the dog greets matters more than any single technique, and most dogs show a real drop in jumping within 2 to 4 weeks when everyone follows the same response. Here's the full method, including how to handle excited guests who undo your training in five seconds.

## Why jumping happens in the first place

Jumping up is a dog's attempt to get closer to a person's face, since that's where the interesting information (scent, eye contact, attention) is concentrated for a dog greeting a human at standing height. It isn't dominance or disrespect, it's an excited greeting behavior that's usually been accidentally reinforced by years of people petting, laughing at, or talking to a jumping dog, even while pushing them off, since pushing still counts as attention and touch to most dogs.

## Step 1: Remove the reward for jumping

The core mechanic: the instant your dog's paws leave the ground toward you, turn your body away, cross your arms, and say nothing. No pushing, no "off," no eye contact. The jump gets zero payoff. As soon as all four paws land back on the floor, even briefly, turn back and calmly reward with attention or a treat.

## Step 2: Reward sit as the default greeting

Rather than only reacting to jumping, proactively teach and reward sitting as the greeting behavior. Practice asking for a sit at the door before you even open it, and reward generously when your dog holds the sit as you or a guest enters. Over time this becomes the dog's default response to an opening door or an approaching person, replacing the jump-first habit.

## Step 3: Manage high-arousal moments

Some situations, an owner coming home after a full workday, an excited child running toward the dog, spike arousal too high for a dog to remember any trained behavior. Use a leash indoors during initial training so you can guide your dog into a sit rather than allowing a full-speed jump rehearsal, and ask excited family members to pause and stand still rather than crouching down and inviting the jump.

## Step 4: Train the "greet guests" sequence separately

Guests are the hardest scenario because they often don't know or follow your training plan, and an excited dog reads a stranger's laughter or petting as a jackpot reward for jumping. Before guests arrive, put your dog on a leash or behind a gate, brief the guest in one sentence ("please ignore her until she's sitting"), and only allow greeting once your dog is calm and seated. Repeating this exact sequence with every visitor, rather than making exceptions for "dog people," is what makes the training stick.

## Comparison of common owner responses

| Owner response | What the dog learns | Effect on jumping |
|---|---|---|
| Push the dog off, say "no" | Touch and voice count as attention | Jumping often continues or increases |
| Ignore completely, turn away | Jumping gets nothing, standing gets attention | Jumping decreases over repeated reps |
| Knee the dog in the chest | Physical discomfort, unpredictable handling | Can create fear or avoidance, not reliable |
| Ask for a sit before greeting | Sitting is the fast path to attention | Reliable calm greetings develop |
| Let kids get excited and jump back | Jumping is a fun mutual game | Jumping intensifies with kids specifically |

## Handling multi-dog households

If more than one dog jumps on guests, train and reward each dog's calm greeting separately before allowing group greetings, since dogs frequently escalate each other's excitement in a way that overrides individual training. Practicing with one dog leashed and one dog in another room, then swapping, builds each dog's independent skill before combining them.

## Realistic timelines

A dog with a mild or newly forming jumping habit often shows clear improvement within 1 to 2 weeks of consistent, whole-household follow-through. A dog with years of reinforced jumping, especially one greeted by many different visitors who don't follow the plan, may take 6 to 8 weeks of consistent practice, and may always show a brief excitement bounce with especially novel or exciting visitors even after training holds for everyday situations.

## Teaching kids to help reinforce the training

Children in the household can meaningfully speed up or slow down jumping training depending on their own consistency. Teach kids the same simple rule adults follow, turn away and ignore jumping, praise four paws on the floor, using calm, simple language they can actually remember and apply in an exciting moment rather than assuming they'll intuitively know what to do.

## FAQ

**Does jumping mean my dog is dominant or badly behaved?**
No. Jumping is an excited greeting behavior, not a dominance display, and most dogs that jump are simply seeking closer contact and attention. Framing it as a training gap rather than a character flaw makes it easier to fix with consistent reward-based responses.

**Why does my dog only jump on certain people?**
Dogs read energy and past reinforcement history. People who've laughed, petted, or talked to the dog during jumping in the past get more of it; people who've consistently ignored jumping and rewarded sitting get calmer greetings. It's about individual reinforcement history, not the dog's opinion of that person.

**Is kneeing a dog in the chest an effective way to stop jumping?**
It isn't recommended. It can cause physical discomfort or injury, produces inconsistent results, and can create fear or unpredictable reactions around handling rather than teaching the dog a reliable alternative behavior like sitting.

**How do I stop my dog from jumping on kids?**
Keep the dog leashed during interactions with children until the sit-greeting is solid, coach kids to stand still and avoid crouching or squealing (which reads as an invitation to jump), and reward the dog specifically for calm behavior around children before allowing off-leash greetings.

**Will my dog ever stop getting excited when I come home?**
The excitement itself is normal and healthy; the goal is redirecting that excitement into an acceptable greeting like sitting rather than eliminating enthusiasm altogether. Most dogs retain some initial bounce but learn to settle into the trained greeting within seconds once the habit is established.

**What if guests keep encouraging the jumping despite my instructions?**
Manage the dog with a leash or gate rather than relying on guest compliance, since a single excited guest can undo weeks of consistent household training in one visit. Brief guests clearly and keep the dog under physical control until you're confident the greeting will go well.

A dog that has made real progress at home but still jumps in novel, high-excitement settings, a pet store, a busy sidewalk, isn't regressing, it's encountering a harder version of the same problem. Treat new environments as a fresh, easier-criteria starting point rather than expecting the trained behavior to transfer instantly at full difficulty.

Bottom line: remove all reward for jumping, whether it's touch, voice, or eye contact, and consistently reward a sit as the alternative greeting. Manage high-arousal situations with a leash and brief guests on the plan, since one inconsistent greeting can slow the whole process.

## Puppies vs adult dogs: does the approach change?

The core mechanic, no reward for jumping, reward for sitting, works for both puppies and adult dogs, but puppies typically respond faster simply because the habit hasn't been reinforced for as many repetitions. An 8-year-old dog that's jumped on every visitor for its entire life has a much longer reinforcement history to work against, which is why consistency matters even more with adult dogs: a single slip where a guest rewards the jump can undo several sessions of progress in a way that matters less for a puppy still forming the habit.

## Building "four on the floor" into daily life, not just training sessions

The fastest way to see jumping fade is to treat every single interaction as a training rep, not just scheduled practice sessions. Every time you approach your dog, kneel down or wait for a sit rather than leaning over and inviting a jump. Every time a family member walks in the door, they follow the same ignore-then-reward sequence. Dogs generalize a rule faster when it's applied with zero exceptions across the full day rather than only during a ten-minute training block, since inconsistent application anywhere in the day effectively teaches the dog that jumping sometimes works.

## What to do if your dog jumps on other dogs, not just people

Some dogs direct the same excited greeting behavior at other dogs on walks or at the park, which reads as rude or overbearing to many dogs even when the intent is friendly. The training principle carries over: reward calm, four-on-the-floor greetings between dogs, interrupt and redirect before the jump happens by watching your dog's body language for the pre-jump crouch-and-spring posture, and practice structured greetings on leash before allowing off-leash dog-to-dog interactions.
