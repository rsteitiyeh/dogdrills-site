The direct answer: lure your dog from a sit into a down by tracking a treat from their nose to the floor and slightly toward their chest, mark the moment their elbows touch the ground, then fade the lure into a hand signal and finally a verbal cue over roughly 5 to 10 short sessions. Most dogs offer a reliable down within one to two weeks of daily 5-minute reps. Here's the full breakdown, including what to do when your dog gets stuck standing up instead of lying down.

## Why the down cue matters more than owners think

Down is one of the four foundation cues the American Kennel Club lists alongside sit, stay, and come, and it does more practical work than most people expect (AKC, akc.org/expert-advice/training). A dog that reliably lies down on cue can settle at a restaurant table, wait calmly during a vet exam, or hold position while you answer the door instead of bolting or jumping. It's also a default behavior you can ask for any time your dog is amped up, since a dog physically cannot lie down and lunge at the same time.

## Step 1: Start from a sit, not a stand

Training down from a standing position is harder because your dog has to fold both the front and back end down at once. Start every rep from a sit. Hold a small, high-value treat right at your dog's nose, then move it straight down to the floor between their front paws. Most dogs will follow the treat down with their head and eventually drop their elbows to keep tracking it.

## Step 2: Handle the "sit-stand-back-up" loop

The most common snag: the dog's head follows the treat down, then their whole body pops back up to standing because the treat kept moving toward you instead of staying put. Fix it by pulling the treat slightly toward their chest and holding it still at floor level once their nose reaches the ground, rather than dragging it away from them. If your dog still won't fold down, try luring them under a low coffee table or your bent knee, which physically blocks the stand-up option and rewards the down position the instant they achieve it.

## Step 3: Mark and reward the exact moment

Timing decides how fast this cue sticks. The reward marker (a clicker or a sharp "yes") needs to land the instant both elbows and the chest touch the floor, not a second later. A marker that's late rewards whatever the dog is doing at that moment, which might be scrambling back up. Deliver the treat right between the paws on the ground for the first several reps, which reinforces staying down rather than popping up to eat it.

## Step 4: Fade the lure into a hand signal, then a word

Once your dog follows the treat into a down 8 or 9 times out of 10, empty your hand and repeat the same downward motion without food in it. Reward from your other hand or a treat pouch after the dog completes the down. After another 5 to 10 successful reps with the empty-hand signal, start saying "down" a half-second before the hand motion. Within a few sessions most dogs anticipate the word and drop before you even move your hand.

## Troubleshooting table

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Dog stands up immediately after lying down | Reward comes late or treat pulls dog forward | Reward on the ground, mark the instant elbows touch |
| Dog won't fold down at all | Reward history too thin, or surface is uncomfortable | Try a soft mat, use higher-value treats, lure under a barrier |
| Dog lies down but rolls onto a hip and gets up early | Cue given without a release word | Add a release cue ("okay") so the dog knows when down ends |
| Dog only responds to the hand signal, not the word | Verbal cue introduced too early | Go back to pairing word and signal together for 5 more reps |
| Dog downs fine at home, ignores cue outside | Distraction level jumped too fast | Practice in the yard, then a quiet sidewalk, before busy areas |

## Building duration and distance

A dog that lies down and pops right back up isn't finished. Add duration gradually: reward after 2 seconds down, then 5, then 10, working up to 30 seconds before you ask for anything else. Add distance the same way, taking one step back before rewarding, then two, building to across-the-room recalls into a down. Rushing duration and distance in the same session is the single biggest reason owners feel like their dog "knows down" at home but ignores it anywhere with distractions.

## How long this actually takes

Most healthy adult dogs and puppies over 12 weeks pick up the lured down within the first two or three sessions. Reliable off-leash response under real distraction (a squirrel, another dog, a doorbell) takes closer to 3 to 6 weeks of consistent daily practice, since that's a generalization problem, not a comprehension problem. Breeds bred for independent work (many terriers, some hounds) often need more reps to build the same reliability that a working or herding breed shows in half the sessions.

## Common mistakes that stall progress

Pushing a dog's shoulders down to force the position teaches avoidance, not compliance, and can create a dog who ducks away from your hands near their back. Repeating the cue word multiple times ("down, down, DOWN") before the dog responds teaches them that the first two don't count. And practicing only at mealtimes with kibble as the sole reward gives you a dog who downs for dinner but not for a walk request, since the reward value never generalizes.

## FAQ

**How long does it take to train a dog to lay down?**
Most dogs learn the lured motion in 2 to 3 short sessions, spread across a couple of days. A dog reliably downing on the first verbal cue with distractions present usually takes 3 to 6 weeks of daily 5-minute practice.

**Why won't my dog lie down on command?**
The most common causes are a reward that arrives too late, a treat that pulls the dog's body forward instead of down, or practicing on a surface the dog finds uncomfortable, like cold tile or gravel. Try a padded mat and reward directly on the ground between the paws.

**Should I push my dog into a down position?**
No. Physically pushing or pressing a dog's back or shoulders can create fear or avoidance around handling and doesn't teach the dog to offer the behavior voluntarily. Luring and rewarding builds a dog who chooses to down because it pays off.

**Can older dogs still learn down?**
Yes. Age doesn't block learning, though dogs with hip or joint discomfort may hesitate to fold down onto hard floors. If an older dog resists despite a comfortable surface and clear luring, a vet check can rule out arthritis or joint pain as the real obstacle.

**What's the difference between down and drop it?**
Down is a body-position cue asking the dog to lie on the floor. Drop it is a release cue for whatever the dog is holding in its mouth. Using the same word for both confuses the dog about which behavior earns the reward, so keep the vocabulary separate.

Bottom line: lure from a sit, reward on the ground the instant elbows touch, fade the food lure into a hand signal and then a word, and build duration and distance separately once the basic motion is solid. Most dogs get a workable down within two weeks; real-world reliability under distraction takes closer to a month of daily short reps.

## Practicing the down cue in different rooms and situations

Dogs don't automatically transfer a skill from one room to another the way people assume. A dog who downs instantly in the kitchen may look confused the first time you ask in the backyard or at a friend's house, not out of stubbornness but because the visual context changed. Run the same 5-minute practice block in at least four or five different locations, starting quiet and low-distraction (a bedroom, a hallway) before moving to busier spots (front yard, a park bench, a pet store entrance). Each new location resets the difficulty slightly, so lower your criteria back to rewarding a fast, complete down before layering duration or distance again.

## Using down as a default calm-down cue

Once the cue is solid, down earns its keep as a default behavior for situations where excitement or reactivity would otherwise take over. Asking for a down when a visitor arrives, before opening the front door, or when another dog passes on a walk gives your dog a clear, physically incompatible alternative to jumping or lunging. This only works if the down has already been proofed with real distractions during practice, so don't wait until a high-stakes moment to test it for the first time.
